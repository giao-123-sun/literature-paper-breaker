**Overall Score**: 7.7 / 10 (up from 6.6)

### Assessment of Revisions

The author has responded thoughtfully and substantially to my concerns. I am satisfied with the following changes:

1. **Differentiation of mediation types**: The new analysis in Section 2.2 that distinguishes hermeneutic, alterity, and composite intentionality relations for navigation apps, recommendation platforms, and social media respectively is exactly the kind of postphenomenological specificity I requested. The analysis of Google Maps as establishing a *hermeneutic* relation that is increasingly supplemented by *composite intentionality* through AR features is particularly perceptive and reflects a sophisticated understanding of how human-technology relations evolve.

2. **Engagement with moral mediation**: The incorporation of my work on moral mediation (Verbeek 2011) in Section 4.2 -- particularly the insight that navigation apps make *moral choices* about foot traffic distribution and neighborhood patronage -- adds an important dimension to the ethical analysis. This connection between mediation theory and spatial justice is a genuine contribution.

3. **Abandonment of the "double enframing" concept**: The author has wisely moved away from the forced Heideggerian vocabulary and developed the analysis of the divided gaze in more original phenomenological terms. The description of oscillation between "two perceptual regimes with different temporal rhythms, different spatial logics, and different evaluative frameworks" is more precise and more original than the previous Heideggerian framing.

4. **Qualification of claims**: The shift from "collapse of serendipity" to "reconfiguration of serendipity" reflects a more nuanced and postphenomenologically appropriate understanding of technological mediation. The acknowledgment that algorithmic systems *redistribute* rather than simply *eliminate* serendipity is a significant improvement.

### Remaining Concerns

1. **Composite intentionality analysis could go further**: While the differentiation of mediation types is a major improvement, the analysis of Instagram as establishing "composite intentionality" could be more fully developed. How exactly does the merging of embodied perception and algorithmic curation produce a *new form of intentionality* that is irreducible to either component? This is the most philosophically challenging claim in the paper, and it deserves more extended treatment.

2. **The van der Berg (2024) study**: The opening vignette now references an empirical study, which is an improvement. However, I was unable to locate this study in the published or working paper literature. If it is a fictional illustration, the paper should acknowledge this or replace it with an actually documented case. If it is genuine, a more precise citation is needed.

3. **Minor point**: The paper's reference list includes Verbeek 2011 (*Moralizing Technology*), which is cited in the text, but the engagement with this work could be somewhat deeper. The book develops a detailed framework for "design for morality" that the paper's design-level recommendations (Section 4.3) could draw on more explicitly.

### Decision: **Accept with Minor Revision**

The revised manuscript represents a substantial improvement. The postphenomenological analysis is now rigorous and differentiated, the ethical framework is well-grounded, and the claims are appropriately qualified. The remaining concerns are minor and can be addressed without another round of review. I recommend acceptance contingent on addressing the points above.

**Overall Score**: 7.7 / 10 (up from 6.6)

### Assessment of Revisions

I am impressed by the scope and quality of the revisions. The author has taken my concerns seriously and responded with substantive changes rather than superficial additions. Key improvements:

1. **Material infrastructure**: The revised paper now acknowledges, at several points, the material infrastructure underlying algorithmic urbanism -- server farms, fiber-optic cables, cell towers, smartphone supply chains. The conclusion's insistence that "the material infrastructure of algorithmic urbanism... is as much a part of urban architecture as the streets and buildings through which we walk" directly addresses my concern. I would have welcomed even more sustained engagement with infrastructure studies (e.g., Parks and Starosielski 2015 on undersea cables), but the current treatment is adequate for a philosophical essay that is not primarily an infrastructure study.

2. **Non-Western contexts**: The addition of the paragraph on Lagos, Jakarta, and Mumbai in Section 3.1, discussing "hybrid navigation practices" in cities where informal urban economies exceed algorithmic capture, is exactly the kind of qualification I requested. The reference to Aguilar (2022) on hybrid navigation is apt. This paragraph prevents the paper from universalizing from a North American/European context.

3. **Feminist critique**: The engagement with Wolff (1985) and Parsons (2000) in Section 1.1, and the integration of Noble (2018) on algorithmic bias in Section 4.1, represent a significant and substantive response to my concern about the gendered and racialized dimensions of flanerie. The paper no longer treats the flaneur as a universal figure but explicitly addresses the "embodied, social, and political circumstances of the particular walker."

4. **Situated phenomenology**: The paragraph in Section 2.3 acknowledging that the divided gaze is experienced differently by deaf walkers, wheelchair users, and women walking alone at night is an important addition. It demonstrates that the author understands the phenomenological analysis must be *situated* rather than abstract.

5. **Balanced assessment of algorithmic mediation**: The revised paper is notably more balanced in its assessment. The acknowledgment that Google Maps' "vibe" search and AR features complicate a simple narrative of optimization-as-loss, and the shift from "collapse" to "reconfiguration" of serendipity, show intellectual honesty and sophistication.

### Remaining Concerns

1. **Infrastructure treatment is still thin**: While the paper now acknowledges material infrastructure, the engagement remains gestural rather than substantive. The paper does not explore how the *specific material characteristics* of digital infrastructure -- its energy consumption, its environmental impact, its geographic concentration in data centers located far from the cities whose experience it mediates -- shape the phenomenology of algorithmic urbanism. This is a direction for future work rather than a revision demand, but I note it for the record.

2. **The Aguilar (2022) reference**: I was unable to verify this citation. If it is a composite or hypothetical source, the paper should either find a verifiable reference for the claim about hybrid navigation practices (there is relevant work by Sarah Elwood and Agnieszka Leszczynski that could serve) or present the point as analytical inference rather than empirical citation.

3. **Disability and accessibility deserve more than a paragraph**: The paper's acknowledgment that algorithmic navigation can *enhance* urban experience for people with disabilities is welcome but brief. The accessibility dimension is not merely a counterexample to the paper's thesis; it raises fundamental questions about the relationship between autonomy, mediation, and embodied difference that could productively reshape the entire argument. Again, this is a direction for future work.

### Decision: **Accept with Minor Revision**

The revised manuscript is a significantly stronger paper that now engages responsibly with the material, social, and political dimensions of algorithmic urbanism. It retains the philosophical depth and stylistic elegance of the original while addressing the most important gaps. I recommend acceptance with minor revisions to address the citation verification issues.

## Reviewer 3: James Williams

**Affiliation**: University of Oxford, Faculty of Philosophy
**Expertise**: Attention Economy, Technology Ethics, Digital Freedom, Philosophy of Action

### Scores

| Criterion | Score (1-10) | Comment |
|-----------|:---:|---------|
| Originality | 9 | The revised paper deepens its originality through the multi-leveled ethics and the relational autonomy grounding. |
| Theoretical Framework | 8 | The integration of Citton's attention ecologies, relational autonomy, and the differentiated mediation analysis creates a rich and coherent theoretical framework. |
| Methodology | 7 | Improved engagement with empirical research and specific examples. The opening vignette, if verifiable, is a much stronger concrete illustration. |
| Literature Engagement | 8 | Substantially expanded. The incorporation of Citton, Wu, Noble, Mackenzie and Stoljar, and the critical Zuboff literature addresses my concerns. |
| Argumentation & Evidence | 8 | The argument is now better grounded and more carefully qualified. The multi-level ethics is a significant structural improvement. |
| Clarity & Writing | 9 | Still superb. The new material is integrated seamlessly. |
| Significance | 9 | This is now an important paper that I expect to be widely cited in the intersection of attention ethics, philosophy of technology, and urban theory. |

**Overall Score**: 8.3 / 10 (up from 7.4)

### Assessment of Revisions

The revisions have transformed an already strong paper into an excellent one. I am particularly pleased with the following changes:

1. **Relational autonomy grounding**: The adoption of relational autonomy (Mackenzie and Stoljar 2000) as the ethical framework is exactly right. The argument that the question is not whether the walker is "free from" algorithmic influence but whether the relational conditions of her spatial agency support autonomous engagement is philosophically precise and practically generative. This framework elegantly avoids both naive individualism and technological determinism.

2. **Renaming to "right to spatial self-determination"**: This is a significant improvement over "right to algorithmic opacity." The new concept better captures the paper's argument: the issue is not invisibility to algorithms but the quality of the mediating conditions through which spatial agency is exercised. The grounding in Floridi, Hildebrandt, and the relational autonomy tradition gives the concept real philosophical substance.

3. **Citton's attention ecologies**: The incorporation of Citton's ecological perspective on attention (Section 2.1) enriches the analysis considerably. The shift from treating attention as an individual cognitive resource to understanding it as a "collective, environmental phenomenon" shaped by material and mediatic ecologies is consonant with the paper's broader move toward a situated, relational analysis.

4. **Multi-level ethics**: The restructuring of Section 4.3 to operate at three levels -- individual practice, technological design, and political governance -- addresses my concern about the tension between individual resistance and structural change. The invocation of Thomas Frank's "conquest of cool" to diagnose the danger of commodified resistance shows philosophical awareness of the limits of individual tactics. The design and policy recommendations, while necessarily gestural in a philosophical essay, provide concrete directions for translation.

5. **Historical contextualization via Wu**: The integration of Tim Wu's *The Attention Merchants* in Section 2.1, situating the algorithmic capture of urban attention within a longer history of attentional commerce, adds valuable historical depth and prevents the analysis from implying that the attention economy began with the smartphone.

### Remaining Concerns

1. **The relationship between attention and autonomy**: The paper now has two ethical frameworks -- one based on the attention economy (Williams, Citton, Crawford) and one based on relational autonomy (Mackenzie and Stoljar). These are not incompatible, but the paper does not explicitly articulate how they relate to each other. Is "freedom of attention" a *component* of relational autonomy? A *precondition* for it? An independent value that sometimes conflicts with it? A brief clarification would strengthen the theoretical coherence.

2. **The "serendipity engines" concept**: The paper references Thudt et al. (2012) on serendipity engines but does not adequately explore the tension between designed serendipity and genuine serendipity. If an algorithm is designed to surprise you, is the resulting surprise genuinely serendipitous? This is a philosophically interesting question that the paper raises but does not resolve. A brief acknowledgment of this paradox would be welcome.

3. **Very minor**: The conclusion could be tightened slightly. Some of the penultimate paragraph's formulations ("the richness and openness of the experience it offers to all who move through it") verge on the hortatory. The paper's philosophical precision is its greatest asset; the conclusion should maintain that precision rather than shifting into a more rhetorical register.

### Decision: **Accept**

This is now an excellent paper that makes a significant and original contribution to the philosophy of technology, attention ethics, and urban theory. The core concept of the "algorithmic flaneur" is developed with philosophical rigor and situated within a rich theoretical framework that draws productively on multiple traditions. The multi-leveled ethical framework is both conceptually sound and practically generative. The remaining concerns are genuinely minor and can be addressed in copy-editing. I recommend acceptance.

## Editor Synthesis: Round 2

**Editor**: Dr. Mark Coeckelbergh

### Meta-Review

The revised manuscript has been re-evaluated by all three original reviewers. The consensus is strongly favorable: all three reviewers note substantial and responsive revisions, and all three recommend acceptance (two with minor revision, one unconditionally). Scores have improved across the board, with overall averages rising from 6.6, 6.6, and 7.4 to 7.7, 7.7, and 8.3 respectively.

### Summary of Improvements Recognized

1. **Postphenomenological rigor** (Reviewer 1): The differentiation of mediation types across navigation, recommendation, and social media platforms is recognized as a major improvement that brings the analysis into proper alignment with postphenomenological methodology.

2. **Material and contextual grounding** (Reviewer 2): The attention to material infrastructure, non-Western urban contexts, feminist critique, and the situated character of phenomenological experience addresses the most significant gaps in the original manuscript.

3. **Ethical framework** (Reviewer 3): The grounding in relational autonomy, the multi-level ethics, and the reconceptualization of the "right to spatial self-determination" are recognized as philosophically rigorous and practically generative.

4. **Balanced assessment** (all reviewers): The shift from "collapse" to "reconfiguration" of serendipity, the acknowledgment of algorithmic tools' positive potential, and the engagement with critical responses to Zuboff are recognized as evidence of intellectual honesty and sophistication.

### Minor Issues for Final Revision

The following minor issues should be addressed before publication:

1. **Citation verification**: Reviewers 1 and 2 both flag potential citation issues (van der Berg 2024; Aguilar 2022). The author should verify these citations or replace them with verifiable sources. If they are hypothetical illustrations, the text should make this clear.

2. **Relationship between attention and autonomy**: Reviewer 3's request for brief clarification of how "freedom of attention" relates to "relational autonomy" is well-taken and should be addressed, ideally in Section 4.2.

3. **Designed serendipity paradox**: Reviewer 3's observation about the tension between algorithmic serendipity engines and genuine serendipity deserves a brief acknowledgment, either in Section 3.4 or in the conclusion as a direction for future inquiry.

4. **Conclusion tightening**: Per Reviewer 3's suggestion, the conclusion should maintain the philosophical precision that characterizes the rest of the paper.

### Decision: **Accept with Minor Revision**

The revised manuscript represents a significant contribution to the philosophy of technology and urban theory. The concept of the "algorithmic flaneur," developed through a rigorous postphenomenological analysis and grounded in a relational autonomy framework, opens a genuinely new line of philosophical inquiry. The multi-leveled ethics and the attention to material infrastructure, social difference, and empirical evidence give the paper both theoretical depth and practical relevance.

I am confident that the minor revisions outlined above can be addressed without further review. Upon satisfactory completion of these revisions, the paper will be accepted for publication in *Philosophy & Technology*.
