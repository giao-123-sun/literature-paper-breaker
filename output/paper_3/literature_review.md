# Literature Review: The Algorithmic Flaneur

## Psychogeography and the Flaneur

1. **Benjamin, Walter. *The Arcades Project*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1999.**
   - Authors: Walter Benjamin (trans. Howard Eiland and Kevin McLaughlin)
   - Year: 1999 (posthumous; written 1927-1940)
   - Publisher: Harvard University Press
   - Summary: Benjamin's unfinished magnum opus examines 19th-century Parisian arcades as sites of commodity culture and modern urban experience. The flaneur emerges as the central figure -- an aesthete wandering the city, observing modernity's kaleidoscopic manifestations. Benjamin treats the flaneur as both product and critic of consumer capitalism, whose disappearance signals the ravages of capitalism on metropolitan life.

2. **Debord, Guy. *The Society of the Spectacle*. New York: Zone Books, 1994 (orig. 1967).**
   - Authors: Guy Debord
   - Year: 1967 (English trans. 1994)
   - Publisher: Zone Books
   - Summary: Debord's foundational Situationist text argues that authentic social life has been replaced by its representation -- "the spectacle." Consumer capitalism reduces lived experience to passive contemplation of images and commodities, producing alienation from genuine social interaction and spatial experience.

3. **Debord, Guy. "Theory of the Derive." *Internationale Situationniste* 2 (1958).**
   - Authors: Guy Debord
   - Year: 1958
   - Publisher: Internationale Situationniste
   - Summary: Debord defines the derive as "a mode of experimental behaviour linked to the conditions of urban society: a technique of rapid passage through varied ambiances." The derive functions as a revolutionary counter-practice to the predictability and monotony of capitalist urban experience, recovering affective dimensions of spatial encounter.

4. **de Certeau, Michel. *The Practice of Everyday Life*. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1984.**
   - Authors: Michel de Certeau
   - Year: 1984
   - Publisher: University of California Press
   - Summary: De Certeau distinguishes between "strategies" (institutional, top-down spatial orderings) and "tactics" (everyday, improvisational practices by which individuals subvert those orderings). His chapter "Walking in the City" treats walking as analogous to speech acts -- the pedestrian actualizes, transgresses, and reinvents spatial possibilities, exercising creative resistance within power structures.

## Phenomenology of Technology

5. **Heidegger, Martin. "The Question Concerning Technology." In *The Question Concerning Technology and Other Essays*. New York: Harper & Row, 1977 (orig. 1954).**
   - Authors: Martin Heidegger
   - Year: 1954 (English trans. 1977)
   - Publisher: Harper & Row
   - Summary: Heidegger argues that modern technology's essence is *Gestell* (Enframing) -- a mode of revealing that reduces all beings to "standing-reserve" (resources for instrumental use). This enframing constitutes the "supreme danger" because it threatens to foreclose other modes of disclosure while simultaneously containing a "saving power" through art and meditative thinking.

6. **Ihde, Don. *Technology and the Lifeworld: From Garden to Earth*. Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1990.**
   - Authors: Don Ihde
   - Year: 1990
   - Publisher: Indiana University Press
   - Summary: Ihde develops postphenomenology as a non-foundational phenomenology analyzing human-technology relations. He identifies four types of technological mediation: embodiment relations (technology as transparent extension), hermeneutic relations (technology as interpretive medium), alterity relations (technology as quasi-other), and background relations (technology as environmental context).

7. **Verbeek, Peter-Paul. *What Things Do: Philosophical Reflections on Technology, Agency, and Design*. University Park: Penn State University Press, 2005.**
   - Authors: Peter-Paul Verbeek
   - Year: 2005
   - Publisher: Penn State University Press
   - Summary: Verbeek extends postphenomenology through his theory of technological mediation, analyzing how technologies actively shape human experience and moral action. He introduces the concept of "cyborg intentionality" -- distinguishing technologically mediated, hybrid, and composite forms of intentionality in human-technology relations.

8. **Verbeek, Peter-Paul. "Cyborg Intentionality: Rethinking the Phenomenology of Human-Technology Relations." *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 7.3 (2008): 387-395.**
   - Authors: Peter-Paul Verbeek
   - Year: 2008
   - Journal: Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences
   - Summary: Verbeek argues for three types of cyborg intentionality involving specific blends of human and technological: technologically mediated intentionality (human intentionality functioning "through" artifacts), hybrid intentionality (technological merging with the human), and composite intentionality (addition of human and technological intentionality).

## Attention Economy and Digital Distraction

9. **Williams, James. *Stand Out of Our Light: Freedom and Resistance in the Attention Economy*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2018.**
   - Authors: James Williams
   - Year: 2018
   - Publisher: Cambridge University Press
   - Summary: Former Google strategist and Oxford philosopher Williams examines how "systems of intelligent persuasion" and "armies of deep distraction" increasingly direct thought and behavior. He classifies three types of ethically problematic distraction -- functional, existential, and epistemic -- and argues for "freedom of attention" as a foundational political value.

10. **Hanin, Mark. "Theorizing Digital Distraction." *Philosophy & Technology* 34 (2021): 395-406.**
    - Authors: Mark Hanin
    - Year: 2021
    - Journal: Philosophy & Technology
    - Summary: Contributes to philosophical reflection on digital distraction by clarifying the nature of the problem in conceptual and historical terms. Analyzes "freedom of attention" as an organizing ideal for moral and political theorizing about the attention economy.

11. **Zuboff, Shoshana. *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power*. New York: PublicAffairs, 2019.**
    - Authors: Shoshana Zuboff
    - Year: 2019
    - Publisher: PublicAffairs
    - Summary: Zuboff defines surveillance capitalism as "the unilateral claiming of private human experience as free raw material for translation into behavioral data." These data are packaged as prediction products sold into behavioral futures markets. She identifies a shift from monitoring to "actuation" -- behavioral modification through "tuning," "herding," and "conditioning" -- mediated by the "Big Other" of digital architecture.

12. **Pariser, Eli. *The Filter Bubble: What the Internet Is Hiding from You*. New York: Penguin Press, 2011.**
    - Authors: Eli Pariser
    - Year: 2011
    - Publisher: Penguin Press
    - Summary: Pariser coins the term "filter bubble" to describe how personalization algorithms trap users in narrowing feedback loops of familiar content, reducing exposure to novel or challenging information. The concept has been widely applied to understand the homogenizing effects of algorithmic recommendation systems on experience and knowledge.

## AI, Navigation, and Urban Experience

13. **McQuire, Scott. "One Map to Rule Them All? Google Maps as Digital Technical Object." *Communication and the Public* 4.2 (2019): 140-153.**
    - Authors: Scott McQuire
    - Year: 2019
    - Journal: Communication and the Public
    - Summary: Analyzes Google Maps as a socio-technical artifact that has fundamentally reconfigured the nexus between technology and spatial experience. Argues that digital maps define our understanding of space more than we define it ourselves, raising concerns about commercial bias, loss of spontaneity, and spatial inequality.

14. **Butler, Michael. "The City, The Highway and the Spatial Level: Navigation Apps and Disorientation." Unpublished manuscript / PhilPapers.**
    - Authors: Michael Butler
    - Year: n.d.
    - Source: PhilPapers
    - Summary: Argues that mobile navigation apps alienate us from lived environments, phenomenologically contrasting the experience of moving through an unfamiliar city on foot versus driving through it with GPS. Highlights "spatial cognitive deskilling" -- how GPS dependency causes the brain to stop building and maintaining mental maps.

15. **Mattern, Shannon. *A City Is Not a Computer: Other Urban Intelligences*. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2021.**
    - Authors: Shannon Mattern
    - Year: 2021
    - Publisher: Princeton University Press
    - Summary: Examines the ethical and ontological implications of urban technologies and computational models. Argues that the "city-as-computer" metaphor reduces place-based knowledge to information processing, and imagines how we might sustain institutions and infrastructures constituting more diverse, open, inclusive urban forms.

16. **Mattern, Shannon. *Code and Clay, Data and Dirt: Five Thousand Years of Urban Media*. Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press, 2017.**
    - Authors: Shannon Mattern
    - Year: 2017
    - Publisher: University of Minnesota Press
    - Summary: Argues that cities have been mediated and intelligent for millennia -- media technologies and their infrastructures have been embedded in and informing the morphological evolution of cities since their origins. Challenges the novelty claims of "smart city" discourse by revealing deep histories of urban mediation.

## Platform Urbanism and Algorithmic Governance

17. **Caprotti, Federico, et al. "Beyond the Smart City: A Typology of Platform Urbanism." *Urban Transformations* 4 (2022): 4.**
    - Authors: Federico Caprotti, Shiuh-Shen Chien, and Hyowon Joo
    - Year: 2022
    - Journal: Urban Transformations
    - Summary: Develops a typology of platform urbanism as an evolution of the smart city concept. Platforms leverage data-centered intermediations to reconfigure urban systems and relationships, reflecting how data has become central to economic organization and platforms act as key mechanisms for structuring social and economic activity.

18. **Cugurullo, Federico, et al. "The Rise of AI Urbanism in Post-Smart Cities: A Critical Commentary on Urban Artificial Intelligence." *Urban Studies* 61.6 (2024): 1113-1126.**
    - Authors: Federico Cugurullo, Federico Caprotti, Matthew Cook, Andrew Karvonen, Pauline McGuirk, and Simon Marvin
    - Year: 2024
    - Journal: Urban Studies
    - Summary: Argues that AI urbanism differs from smart urbanism in theory and practice. In AI urbanism, the focus is on prediction and anticipation -- autonomous cars anticipating busy junctions, city brains foreseeing energy consumption, algorithms predicting property values or crime -- raising new questions about agency, governance, and urban futures.

19. **Greenfield, Adam. *Radical Technologies: The Design of Everyday Life*. London: Verso, 2017.**
    - Authors: Adam Greenfield
    - Year: 2017
    - Publisher: Verso
    - Summary: Examines how technologies including smartphones, augmented reality, digital fabrication, cryptocurrency, and the Internet of Things are reshaping daily life and urban experience. Offers critical analysis of "smart city" ideology and the ways in which algorithmic systems structure spatial practices and social relations.

20. **Crawford, Matthew. *The World Beyond Your Head: On Becoming an Individual in an Age of Distraction*. New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2015.**
    - Authors: Matthew Crawford
    - Year: 2015
    - Publisher: Farrar, Straus and Giroux
    - Summary: Develops an "ethics of attention" arguing that our attentional environment is a political issue. Crawford treats attention as a resource under siege by commercial interests, connecting the colonization of attention to broader questions about individual agency, skilled practice, and the phenomenology of engagement with the material world.
