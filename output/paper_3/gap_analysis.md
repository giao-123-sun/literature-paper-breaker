# Gap Analysis: The Algorithmic Flaneur

## Identified Research Gaps

### Gap 1: The Absence of a Postphenomenological Account of Algorithmic Navigation as Psychogeographic Practice

Existing scholarship on psychogeography (Benjamin, Debord, de Certeau) remains largely anchored in pre-digital urban contexts. Meanwhile, postphenomenological analyses of technology (Ihde, Verbeek) have not systematically addressed how navigation and recommendation algorithms mediate spatial experience in the specific tradition of psychogeographic thought. There is no sustained philosophical account that bridges Debord's derive with Ihde's human-technology relations to analyze how GPS-mediated walking constitutes a new form of psychogeographic practice -- one that is neither the free drift of the Situationists nor the purely instrumental traversal of space.

**Significance**: This gap leaves us without conceptual tools for understanding the phenomenological transformation wrought by algorithmic navigation on the very practice (urban walking) that psychogeography takes as its object.

### Gap 2: The Attention Economy as Spatial Architecture

While the attention economy has been analyzed extensively in relation to digital media, social platforms, and epistemic agency (Williams 2018, Hanin 2021, Crawford 2015), there is remarkably little work connecting attention economics to the phenomenology of urban space. How does the colonization of attention by smartphones and location-based services restructure the lived experience of cities? The attention economy literature treats attention primarily as a temporal resource (time spent on screens), but neglects its fundamentally spatial dimensions -- how attentional capture by digital interfaces reshapes our perceptual relationship to the built environment.

**Significance**: Bridging attention economics with urban phenomenology would reveal how algorithmic systems function as a new kind of spatial architecture -- not built from stone and steel, but from notification patterns, interface designs, and recommendation flows that redirect the embodied attention of the urban dweller.

### Gap 3: Serendipity as a Phenomenological and Political Category

The filter bubble and serendipity literature (Pariser 2011) remains largely confined to information science and recommendation system design. It has not been adequately theorized as a phenomenological category -- a mode of encounter with the world that has ontological significance. The Situationist concept of the derive was precisely an attempt to cultivate serendipitous encounter, but no one has systematically analyzed how algorithmic recommendation systems (Yelp, Google Maps, Instagram location features) constitute an anti-derive -- a systematic foreclosure of the unexpected that has implications not only for information diversity but for the very structure of urban experience.

**Significance**: Treating serendipity as a phenomenological category (rather than merely an information-theoretic property) would connect the technical literature on recommendation systems to the philosophical tradition of psychogeography and existential phenomenology.

### Gap 4: The Flaneur as Critical Figure for Platform Urbanism

Platform urbanism scholarship (Caprotti et al. 2022, Cugurullo et al. 2024) has developed sophisticated analyses of how digital platforms restructure urban governance, economy, and infrastructure. However, this literature lacks a phenomenological account of the lived experience of platform-mediated urbanism from the perspective of the urban subject. The figure of the flaneur -- historically the paradigmatic urban subject -- has not been mobilized as a critical lens for understanding what it means to inhabit a platform city.

**Significance**: Recovering and updating the flaneur figure would provide platform urbanism studies with a phenomenological vocabulary for the experiential dimension of algorithmic urban governance, complementing its existing focus on political economy and infrastructure.

### Gap 5: An Ethics of Algorithmic Wandering

While Zuboff (2019) provides a comprehensive political economy of surveillance capitalism and its behavioral modification techniques, and Verbeek (2005, 2008) develops an ethics of technological mediation, there is no sustained ethical framework specifically addressing the algorithmic determination of spatial experience. What are the ethical stakes of having one's path through a city determined by Google Maps' optimization algorithms? What is lost when Yelp ratings replace the chance encounter with an unknown restaurant? Existing work in the ethics of AI tends to focus on bias, fairness, and transparency, but neglects the phenomenological and existential dimensions of algorithmic mediation of spatial practice.

**Significance**: Developing an ethics of algorithmic wandering would contribute to both philosophy of technology and urban ethics by articulating what is at stake -- existentially, politically, phenomenologically -- when algorithms increasingly determine how and where we move through cities.
