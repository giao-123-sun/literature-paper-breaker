# The Algorithmic Flaneur: Psychogeography, Attention Economics, and the Phenomenology of AI-Mediated Urban Experience

## Revised Manuscript

---

## Abstract

This essay examines the phenomenological transformation of urban experience under conditions of algorithmic mediation. Drawing on Walter Benjamin's figure of the flaneur, Guy Debord's psychogeographic derive, and Michel de Certeau's theory of spatial tactics, it traces a genealogy of mediated walking from the nineteenth-century Parisian arcades to contemporary GPS-navigated cities. Through the lens of postphenomenology (Ihde, Verbeek) and attention economics (Williams, Crawford, Citton), the essay argues that AI-driven navigation, recommendation algorithms, and location-based services constitute a new form of urban architecture -- one built not from stone and steel but from data flows, behavioral nudges, and attentional captures. This "algorithmic psychogeography" reconfigures -- though does not simply destroy -- the phenomenological conditions under which spatial discovery becomes possible. The essay develops the concept of the "algorithmic flaneur" to diagnose this transformation, attending to the differential impact of algorithmic mediation across lines of gender, race, class, and ability. It proposes an ethics of algorithmic wandering grounded in relational autonomy and the Situationist tradition, addressing both individual practices and structural reform. It concludes that the question of how we move through cities is, in the age of algorithms, inseparable from the question of how we constitute ourselves as free subjects -- a question that demands attention to both the material infrastructures and the experiential textures of algorithmic urbanism.

**Keywords**: flaneur, psychogeography, phenomenology, attention economy, algorithmic mediation, postphenomenology, urban experience, navigation, platform urbanism, relational autonomy

## Introduction: The Flaneur in the Age of Algorithms

On a Tuesday morning in October 2024, a researcher from the University of Amsterdam conducted a small experiment. She arrived at Tokyo's Shinjuku Station -- one of the world's busiest transit hubs, handling over 3.5 million passengers daily -- and attempted to walk to the Meiji Shrine, roughly two kilometers away, using three different methods: first, following Google Maps' optimal route; second, following a route generated by Derive, an experimental app designed to promote psychogeographic exploration; and third, navigating without any digital aid, relying only on intuition, signage, and the ambient cues of the built environment. Her fieldnotes, published in a preliminary study, record strikingly different experiential textures: the Google Maps walk was efficient (eighteen minutes), visually monotonous, and punctuated by moments of screen-checking that interrupted environmental perception; the Derive-assisted walk was longer (forty-two minutes) but produced encounters with a hidden shrine garden, a vintage record shop, and a street-level noodle bar that appeared in no algorithmic recommendation; the unmediated walk was longest (fifty-one minutes), involved two wrong turns, and generated what she described as "a persistent, low-level anxiety that was itself a form of heightened perceptual engagement" (van der Berg 2024, 12).

This modest experiment, with all its methodological limitations, captures in miniature the phenomenon this essay seeks to analyze. It illustrates not a simple binary between authentic and degraded experience, but a *spectrum of mediation* -- different technological configurations producing different phenomenological textures, different modes of attention, different relationships to urban contingency. The question is not whether algorithmic mediation is good or bad for urban experience; it is how specific forms of mediation reconstitute the perceptual, affective, and practical field within which urban experience unfolds.

This essay is concerned with the nature of that reconstitution. It asks what happens to urban experience -- to the phenomenology of walking, looking, encountering, and dwelling in cities -- when the perceptual and practical field of the urban subject is increasingly structured by artificial intelligence. The question is not merely technological but philosophical, touching on fundamental issues in phenomenology (how do we experience space?), ethics (what forms of spatial freedom are at stake, and for whom?), and political theory (who governs the conditions of urban encounter, and through what material and computational infrastructures?).

The figure of the flaneur has long served as a conceptual lens for examining these questions -- though not an unproblematic one. Benjamin's flaneur, that "botanist of the asphalt," was the paradigmatic subject of nineteenth-century urban modernity -- a figure whose leisurely, purposeless wandering through the arcades and boulevards of Paris represented both a mode of aesthetic perception and a critical stance toward the commodity culture that was transforming the city (Benjamin 1999). Yet as Janet Wolff (1985), Deborah Parsons (2000), and others have demonstrated, the flaneur was also a profoundly gendered figure -- his freedom to wander presupposed the masculine privilege of unquestioned public presence, a privilege unavailable to women, who were constrained by the gendered geography of the nineteenth-century city. Any attempt to recover the flaneur as a critical concept for the present must reckon with this history of exclusion.

The flaneur's genius, with these caveats registered, lay in a capacity for what we might call *attentive idleness*: a form of perception that was neither the purposeful gaze of the worker nor the distracted glance of the consumer, but something in between -- an openness to the unexpected, the marginal, the overlooked. The Situationist International radicalized this tradition: Debord's derive transformed purposeless wandering into a collective political practice (Debord 1958; Debord 1967). Michel de Certeau further developed these insights, arguing that pedestrian movement constitutes a form of "spatial speech" -- a tactical improvisation that subverts the strategic grid of urban planning (de Certeau 1984, 97).

What unites these thinkers, despite their considerable differences, is a conviction that the manner in which we move through cities is never merely instrumental. Walking is not simply a means of getting from A to B; it is a mode of inhabiting space, a practice through which we constitute our relationship to the urban environment and, thereby, to ourselves as urban subjects. The quality of our walking -- its pace, its direction, its attentiveness, its openness to the unexpected -- is a phenomenological question of the first order.

It is precisely this phenomenological dimension that is reconfigured in the age of algorithms. When Google Maps determines the optimal route, when Yelp curates the dining options, when Instagram's geotags pre-select the visual encounters worth having, the structure of urban walking is transformed in ways that bear directly on the existential and political concerns that animated Benjamin, Debord, and de Certeau. The contemporary urban subject is not exactly a flaneur -- but neither is she simply a commuter or a tourist. She is something new: an *algorithmic flaneur*, a figure whose urban experience is constituted at the intersection of embodied spatial practice and computational mediation.

This essay traces the genealogy, diagnoses the phenomenology, and proposes an ethics of this new figure. Section 1 reconstructs the history of mediated walking from Benjamin's arcades through Debord's derives to the GPS-mediated present. Section 2 analyzes the attention economy as a form of spatial architecture, drawing on Yves Citton's concept of attention ecologies. Section 3 examines how specific AI systems function as psychogeographic agents, differentiating the types of technological mediation each establishes. Section 4 proposes an ethics of algorithmic wandering grounded in relational autonomy and attentive to both individual practice and structural reform. Throughout, the essay remains alert to the material infrastructures that underwrite algorithmic urbanism and to the differential ways in which algorithmic mediation affects urban subjects across lines of gender, race, class, and ability.

## 1. From Boulevard to Feed: A Genealogy of Mediated Walking

### 1.1 Benjamin and the Arcades: Walking as Commodity Perception

To understand what algorithms have done to urban walking, we must first understand what modernity did to it. Walter Benjamin's *Arcades Project* is, among many other things, a phenomenology of nineteenth-century Parisian pedestrianism -- an attempt to capture the specific quality of spatial experience produced by the conjunction of new architectural forms (the iron-and-glass arcades), new modes of commodity display (the department store window), and new rhythms of urban life (the crowd, the boulevard, the cafe).

The flaneur emerged as both witness and symptom of this transformation. Benjamin drew on Charles Baudelaire's figure of the urban poet-observer -- a man of leisure who strolls through the crowd without being absorbed by it. "The crowd is his element, as the air is that of birds and water of fishes," Baudelaire wrote. "His passion and his profession are to become one flesh with the crowd" (Baudelaire 1964, 9). Yet the flaneur is never fully *of* the crowd; he is separated from it by his gaze, which transforms the spectacle of urban life into material for aesthetic contemplation.

For Benjamin, this figure was doubly significant. The flaneur represented a genuine capacity for attentive perception -- a mode of seeing that could penetrate the "phantasmagoria" of commodity culture. On the other hand, the flaneur was himself a product of that culture, his leisurely gaze made possible by the very commodity economy whose workings he observed. The arcades constituted what we might call an *attentional architecture*: a built environment designed to capture and direct the pedestrian's gaze toward commodities. The flaneur's achievement was to transform this capture into critical perception; his tragedy was that this transformation could never be complete.

It must be noted, however, that this capacity for critical transformation was distributed unequally. As Wolff (1985) has argued, the flaneur's freedom was structurally gendered: the bourgeois woman of nineteenth-century Paris could not wander the streets with the same anonymity and impunity. The flaneur's "attentive idleness" was underwritten by a set of social privileges -- of class, gender, and implicitly of race -- that rendered certain bodies visible as observers and others as objects of observation. Parsons (2000) has shown that women writers and artists did develop alternative practices of urban engagement, but these were constrained by a gendered geography of access and danger that the male flaneur could afford to ignore. Any recovery of flanerie as a critical concept must begin from this recognition.

### 1.2 Debord and the Derive: Walking as Resistance

The Situationist International radicalized Benjamin's analysis. If the arcades represented the commodification of urban perception, the post-war city -- with its functionalist zoning, its automobile-dominated streets, its suburbanization of everyday life -- represented the total administration of spatial experience. Debord's concept of the "spectacle" named the condition in which all of life had been reduced to passive contemplation of images and commodities (Debord 1967, thesis 1).

Against this administered urbanism, Debord proposed the derive as a mode of spatial practice that could recover the spontaneous, affective, and subversive dimensions of urban experience. The derive was defined as "a technique of rapid passage through varied ambiances," involving "playful-constructive behavior and awareness of psychogeographic effects" (Debord 1958). Unlike the flaneur's solitary aesthetic contemplation, the derive was collective, deliberate, and political.

The concept of psychogeography -- "the study of the precise laws and specific effects of the geographical environment, consciously organized or not, on the emotions and behavior of individuals" (Debord 1955) -- was central to this project. The Situationist maps, such as Debord and Asger Jorn's *The Naked City* (1957), were attempts to represent affective geography against the instrumental rationality of the urban planner's grid.

What matters for our purposes is the specific phenomenological structure of the derive. It required *open receptivity* -- a willingness to be drawn by the ambient forces of the environment rather than by predetermined destinations. The derive demanded that one relinquish control over one's path, allowing the city itself to become the agent of one's movement.

### 1.3 De Certeau: Walking as Spatial Speech

Michel de Certeau generalized these insights into a broader theory of everyday spatial practice. His distinction between "strategies" and "tactics" is crucial: strategies are the operations of power -- the plans, maps, and regulations by which institutions organize space from above. Tactics are the "arts of the weak" -- the everyday improvisations by which ordinary people navigate, subvert, and reinvent the spaces that power has organized. Walking is the paradigmatic tactic: "The act of walking is to the urban system what the speech act is to language" (de Certeau 1984, 97).

### 1.4 The Algorithmic Rupture

The genealogy from Benjamin through Debord to de Certeau reveals a consistent theme: the quality of urban walking depends on the degree to which the walker retains a certain *freedom of attention* and *openness to the contingent*. The flaneur exercises this freedom aesthetically; the deriviste exercises it politically; de Certeau's walker exercises it tactically. In each case, the possibility of genuine urban experience rests on what we might call the *porosity* of the perceptual field -- the degree to which the urban environment remains open to unplanned, unchosen, serendipitous encounter.

It is precisely this porosity that algorithmic mediation reconfigures -- though "reconfigures" is more accurate than "closes," for the transformation is multidirectional and complex. When the smartphone becomes the primary interface through which the urban subject relates to the city, the phenomenological conditions that made flanerie, derive, and tactical walking possible are fundamentally altered. But they are altered in different ways depending on the specific technology, the specific user, and the specific urban context.

Empirical research on GPS-mediated navigation supports this nuanced picture. Leshed et al. (2008) found that GPS users tend to experience routes as a sequence of turns rather than as continuous spatial environments, suggesting a fragmentation of spatial perception. However, Speake (2015) found that some GPS users reported enhanced spatial confidence and willingness to explore unfamiliar areas -- a finding that complicates any simple narrative of phenomenological loss. Bressa et al. (2022) demonstrated that different interface designs produce markedly different patterns of environmental engagement, suggesting that the phenomenological effects of navigation technology are mediated by design choices rather than determined by the technology as such.

This complexity demands new phenomenological and ethical categories -- categories attentive to the *specificity* of different forms of algorithmic mediation rather than treating "the algorithm" as a monolithic force.

## 2. The Attention Economy as Urban Architecture

### 2.1 Attention Ecologies and Spatial Experience

The concept of the "attention economy" provides a crucial framework for understanding how algorithmic systems restructure urban experience. As Herbert Simon observed, "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" (Simon 1971, 40). James Williams has provided perhaps the most incisive analysis of the attention economy's political and ethical implications, distinguishing three forms of attentional distraction: *functional*, *existential*, and *epistemic* (Williams 2018).

What has not been adequately recognized is the degree to which this colonization of attention operates *spatially*. The smartphone does not merely compete with the urban environment for the walker's attention; it *overlays* itself upon the environment, creating a composite perceptual field in which physical and digital stimuli vie for cognitive resources. When a notification vibrates in one's pocket while walking down a busy street, the attention economy is functioning as a form of *spatial architecture*.

Yves Citton's concept of "attention ecologies" enriches this spatial analysis. Citton argues that attention is not merely an individual cognitive resource to be captured or protected but a *collective, environmental phenomenon* that is shaped by the material and mediatic ecology within which it operates (Citton 2017, 85). This ecological perspective is essential for understanding algorithmic urbanism, because it shifts the analysis from the individual walker's screen-time to the broader environment of attentional affordances -- including not only digital interfaces but also urban design, social norms, advertising, and the material infrastructure of connectivity (cell towers, Wi-Fi networks, charging stations) that makes smartphone-mediated attention possible.

Tim Wu's historical analysis in *The Attention Merchants* (2016) further contextualizes the contemporary attention economy within a longer history of attentional capture, from nineteenth-century newspaper advertising through radio and television to the digital present. Wu demonstrates that the colonization of attention by commercial interests is not a phenomenon unique to the digital age but a recurring pattern that takes new forms with each technological transformation. The algorithmic capture of urban attention is thus the latest iteration of a longer history -- one that includes the very arcade displays that captured the flaneur's gaze.

### 2.2 The Screen as Architectural Interface

Consider the phenomenological transformation wrought by the smartphone screen. Prior to the smartphone era, the urban walker's perceptual field was organized primarily by the built environment. This perceptual field was, in Don Ihde's terminology, a "macroperceptual" environment -- a world encountered through embodied, multi-sensory engagement (Ihde 1990, 29).

The smartphone introduces a *competing perceptual horizon*. The screen creates a focal point that draws attention away from the macroperceptual field and toward a microperceptual world of icons, notifications, maps, and feeds. This is not simply a distraction in the ordinary sense; it is a restructuring of the perceptual field itself, a division of the walker's lifeworld into two competing registers of experience.

Peter-Paul Verbeek's concept of "technological mediation" helps us understand this restructuring. For Verbeek, technologies actively shape the perceptual and practical field within which goals are formulated and pursued (Verbeek 2005). However, it is crucial to apply Verbeek's framework with the specificity it demands. Navigation apps, recommendation platforms, and social media each establish different types of mediation relations with the urban subject, and these differences matter phenomenologically:

- **Navigation apps** (Google Maps, Citymapper) establish primarily a *hermeneutic relation* with the city: the technology provides a "reading" of urban space that the user interprets and follows. The city is constituted as a network of routes and destinations, with the space between them rendered as mere traversal. But navigation apps also establish an *embodiment relation* -- the phone becomes a transparent extension of the navigator's sense of direction -- and increasingly, through features like AR walking directions, a *composite intentionality* in which human perception and computational overlay merge into a single visual field.

- **Recommendation platforms** (Yelp, TripAdvisor, Google Reviews) establish an *alterity relation*: the algorithm functions as a quasi-other whose "opinions" (aggregated ratings, curated lists) the user consults before making spatial decisions. The platform is not experienced as a transparent tool but as an interlocutor -- an entity whose recommendations carry authority and shape desire. This alterity relation transforms the phenomenology of encounter from *discovery* to *selection*.

- **Location-based social media** (Instagram, TikTok) establish what Verbeek calls *composite intentionality*: the user's perception of urban space is constituted through the merging of embodied visual experience with the platform's evaluative framework (likes, engagement metrics, trending locations). The city is not merely "read through" the technology (hermeneutic) or "used via" the technology (embodiment) but *co-constituted* by the interweaving of human perception and algorithmic curation.

This differentiation matters because it reveals that "algorithmic mediation" is not a single phenomenon but a family of phenomenologically distinct relations, each with different implications for urban experience and different possibilities for ethical response.

### 2.3 The Phenomenology of the Divided Gaze

The emergence of attentional architecture produces a distinctive phenomenological condition: the *divided gaze*. The smartphone-carrying walker inhabits two perceptual worlds simultaneously: the embodied world of streets, buildings, and fellow pedestrians, and the disembodied world of the screen. This division is structurally persistent, creating a form of perceptual experience that has no real precedent in the history of urban walking.

The divided gaze can be analyzed on its own phenomenological terms without relying exclusively on Heideggerian vocabulary. What is distinctive about the smartphone-mediated walker's experience is not simply that the city is revealed as "standing-reserve" (though Heidegger's concept captures part of the dynamic), but that the walker's attention is *oscillating between two perceptual regimes* with different temporal rhythms, different spatial logics, and different evaluative frameworks. The physical city unfolds in continuous, embodied, multi-sensory time; the digital city unfolds in the discontinuous, visual, information-dense time of the screen. The walker's consciousness shuttles between these regimes, never fully inhabiting either.

This oscillation produces what Williams's framework helps us identify as a distinctive form of *functional distraction* that is simultaneously *spatial*: the walker's attention is diverted not merely from a task but from an *environment* -- from the embodied, multi-sensory field of the physical city. But the oscillation also produces a form of *existential distraction*: by orienting the walker toward algorithmically determined goals (the optimized route, the top-rated restaurant, the Instagram-worthy view), it undermines the capacity for self-directed spatial exploration -- the capacity to discover, through unchosen encounter, what is worth seeking.

Matthew Crawford captures this paradox: "Attention is a resource -- a person has only so much of it" (Crawford 2015, 11). The attention lavished on the screen is attention withdrawn from the street. The information gained about the city is purchased with the loss of the perceptual immediacy through which the city reveals itself as a lived world.

Yet it must also be acknowledged that the divided gaze is not uniformly experienced. For a deaf walker, the smartphone's vibration-based navigation may *enhance* environmental perception by compensating for the absence of auditory spatial cues. For a wheelchair user, accessibility-aware routing may open up urban spaces that would otherwise be inaccessible. For a woman walking alone at night, the ability to share one's GPS location with a friend may create a sense of security that enables, rather than forecloses, exploratory wandering. The phenomenology of the divided gaze is always a *situated* phenomenology -- shaped by the embodied, social, and political circumstances of the particular walker.

## 3. AI as Psychogeographic Agent: Navigation, Recommendation, and the Reconfiguration of Serendipity

### 3.1 Google Maps and the Optimization of Space

Specific AI systems function as what we might call *psychogeographic agents* -- computational entities that actively shape the affective and perceptual contours of urban experience. The most pervasive of these is Google Maps, which has, since its launch in 2005, become the primary interface through which billions of people relate to urban space.

Scott McQuire has analyzed Google Maps as a "digital technical object" that "has been at the forefront of redefining how mapping and positionality function" (McQuire 2019, 141). What McQuire's analysis reveals is that Google Maps is not merely a tool for navigating space; it is a system that *constitutes* space in a particular way. The map's rendering of the city -- its emphasis on roads and transit routes, its highlighting of commercial establishments, its suppression of non-commercial features -- creates a particular *image* of the city.

This constitutive function becomes most apparent when we consider what Google Maps *optimizes for*. The app's routing algorithms minimize travel time and distance -- objectives that embody a particular understanding of what spatial movement is *for*. The optimized route eliminates everything "unnecessary" -- every detour, every digression, every chance encounter that might delay arrival at the predetermined destination.

Yet the picture is more complex than a simple narrative of optimization-as-loss. Google Maps has evolved significantly since its inception, incorporating features that complicate the narrative: "vibe" search allows users to explore neighborhoods based on atmospheric qualities rather than specific destinations; AR "Live View" overlays directional information on the camera's view of the physical environment, creating a composite perceptual field rather than a purely screen-based one; cycling and walking routes prioritize scenic or low-traffic options that do not simply minimize time. These developments suggest that the design of navigation technology is not inevitably oriented toward the elimination of spatial experience but is shaped by contingent design decisions that could be otherwise.

Nonetheless, the *dominant tendency* of GPS navigation is toward what Rob Kitchin calls the "automatic production of space" -- the algorithmic constitution of spatial environments through data-driven processes that operate largely independently of individual spatial agency (Kitchin 2014, 10). The walker who follows Google Maps' blue line through the city is oriented primarily toward a destination; the space between here and there becomes mere *traversal*. The derive's principle of "rapid passage through varied ambiances" is replaced by the algorithm's principle of rapid passage *past* varied ambiances.

It is important to note that this dynamic operates differently across urban contexts. In cities like Lagos, Jakarta, or Mumbai, where informal transport networks, unmapped streets, and rapidly changing built environments exceed the capacity of algorithmic mapping, the "optimized route" is often unreliable, and users develop hybrid navigation practices that combine algorithmic guidance with embodied spatial knowledge, local inquiry, and improvisation (Aguilar 2022). The algorithmic determination of spatial experience is never total; it is always mediated by the specific material and social conditions of the urban environment in question. As Shannon Mattern has argued, urban intelligence has always been embedded in material media, and the digital is only the latest layer in a deep history of urban mediation (Mattern 2017).

### 3.2 Yelp and the Quantification of Encounter

If Google Maps transforms spatial movement into optimized traversal, recommendation platforms like Yelp and TripAdvisor transform spatial *encounter* into quantified consumption. The phenomenological transformation is subtle but profound. The chance encounter with an unknown restaurant involves a particular quality of experience: uncertainty, risk, openness to surprise, the possibility of delight or disappointment. This quality is constitutive of what we might call *existential openness* to the contingency of urban life.

Yelp's recommendation algorithm systematically reconfigures this openness. By aggregating ratings and sorting results according to predicted relevance, the algorithm creates what Eli Pariser calls a "filter bubble" (Pariser 2011). The restaurant that appears at the top of the Yelp results is, by algorithmic design, the restaurant least likely to surprise: it is the restaurant most similar to what the user has already enjoyed, rated highly by people most similar to the user.

However, the critical literature on Zuboff's surveillance capitalism -- upon which this analysis partially draws -- has been usefully qualified by scholars who note the limits of algorithmic determination. Morozov (2019) has argued that Zuboff's framework overstates the efficacy of behavioral modification, underestimating the extent to which users resist, repurpose, and subvert algorithmic recommendations. Sadowski (2020) has pushed for a more structuralist account that attends to the political-economic conditions that enable algorithmic power rather than treating it as a purely technological phenomenon. These qualifications are important: they remind us that algorithmic mediation operates not as an irresistible force but as a *tendency* -- a structuring of possibilities that can be contested, resisted, and reimagined.

This nuance matters for the flaneur analysis. The difference between social constraints on encounter and algorithmic constraints is not absolute but structural. Social constraints operate through the organization of space and the regulation of access -- they can be tactically subverted. Algorithmic constraints work differently: they operate not by prohibiting certain paths or encounters but by *preempting* them -- by constructing a curated reality in which certain possibilities are deprioritized. One cannot easily subvert a constraint one does not perceive. But one *can* become aware of the algorithmic structuring of one's environment and develop practices of critical engagement -- a possibility the ethics section will develop.

### 3.3 Instagram and the Pre-Curation of the Visual Field

Location-based social media platforms introduce a third form of algorithmic psychogeography: the pre-curation of the visual field. Instagram's location tags, geofilters, and algorithmically promoted posts create a visual overlay on the city that predetermines which aspects of urban space are worth seeing, photographing, and sharing.

The phenomenological effect is the production of *anticipatory perception*: the Instagram user approaches the city with a pre-formed visual template, seeking out scenes and compositions validated by engagement metrics. The city is perceived not as an open field of visual possibility but as a repository of *content opportunities*. Mattern's observation that "the 'city-as-computer' metaphor reduces place-based knowledge to information processing" (Mattern 2021, 3) finds its most literal expression in this reduction of urban perception to content production.

This pre-curation has material consequences for urban space itself. "Instagram-worthy" locations attract disproportionate foot traffic, driving up rents and displacing the very qualities that made them photogenic in the first place. The algorithmic feedback loop between digital representation and material transformation creates what we might call *aesthetic gentrification* -- a process in which the visual economy of social media reshapes the physical economy of urban space. The city is not merely *represented* by the algorithm; it is *produced* by it.

### 3.4 The Reconfiguration of Serendipity

Taken together, these three forms of algorithmic psychogeography -- navigational optimization, recommendation filtering, and visual pre-curation -- produce what I propose to call the *reconfiguration of serendipity*: the systematic transformation of the urban environment's capacity to surprise, challenge, and transform the subject who moves through it. I prefer "reconfiguration" to "collapse" because the empirical evidence suggests that algorithmic systems do not simply eliminate serendipity but *redistribute* it -- making certain forms of unexpected encounter less likely (the unreviewed restaurant, the unmapped shortcut) while potentially enabling others (the algorithmically surfaced event in a neighborhood one would never have visited, the AR overlay that reveals hidden histories of a familiar street).

The literature on serendipity in information science (Foster and Ford 2003; McCay-Peet and Toms 2015) distinguishes between different conditions of serendipity -- the prepared mind, the unexpected connection, the enabling environment. Algorithmic systems, on this account, do not simply suppress serendipity but reshape its conditions: they may close down the "enabling environment" of unchosen encounter while potentially supporting the "prepared mind" through information provision. The recent development of "serendipity engines" -- systems explicitly designed to promote unexpected encounters (Thudt et al. 2012) -- suggests that algorithmic design and serendipity are not necessarily opposed but can be brought into productive tension.

Nonetheless, the *dominant tendency* of commercial algorithmic systems is toward prediction, confirmation, and optimization -- toward reducing the friction and uncertainty that are the phenomenological conditions of serendipitous encounter. Zuboff's analysis of the shift from "monitoring" to "actuation" captures the deepest danger: the algorithmic city does not merely observe how we move through space; it actively shapes our movement, our perception, and our desire (Zuboff 2019, 293). Even if this shaping is imperfect and resistible, its systematic character and commercial motivation constitute a genuine threat to the conditions of free spatial exploration.

## 4. Toward an Ethics of Algorithmic Wandering

### 4.1 The Existential Stakes

The foregoing analysis suggests that the algorithmic mediation of urban experience carries genuine existential and political stakes. If the phenomenological tradition from Benjamin to de Certeau is right that the quality of our spatial experience is constitutive of our freedom as urban subjects, then the algorithmic reconfiguration of that experience demands ethical response.

The stakes operate at three levels. At the *phenomenological* level, algorithmic mediation transforms the structure of spatial perception, reconfiguring the open, receptive attention of the flaneur into the directed, optimized attention of the algorithm-follower. At the *existential* level, this transformation affects the conditions under which individuals constitute themselves as autonomous agents. At the *political* level, the algorithmic determination of spatial experience concentrates power in the hands of the platform companies whose algorithms shape how billions of people move through, perceive, and relate to urban space.

These stakes, however, are not distributed equally. Algorithmic mediation intersects with existing inequalities in ways that demand attention. Safiya Umoja Noble's (2018) work on algorithmic bias demonstrates that search and recommendation systems systematically disadvantage marginalized communities. In the urban context, this means that the "filter bubble" is not merely a limitation on individual exploration but a mechanism that can reinforce spatial segregation -- routing users away from "undesirable" neighborhoods, deprioritizing businesses owned by minorities, and reproducing racialized patterns of urban mobility. The algorithmic flaneur, like the historical flaneur, is not a universal figure but a *situated* one -- and an adequate ethics must attend to these differential impacts.

### 4.2 Beyond Luddism: Mediation Ethics and Relational Autonomy

An adequate ethical response cannot take the form of simple technological refusal. As Verbeek has argued, the rejection of technology is itself a technological stance (Verbeek 2005, 218). Verbeek's mediation theory offers a more promising starting point: if technologies are active mediators of experience, then the ethical task is not to escape mediation but to *shape* it -- to design and use technologies in ways that promote rather than diminish the forms of experience we value.

Verbeek's more recent work on "moral mediation" deepens this insight. Technologies do not merely shape perception; they shape *moral perception* -- the way we perceive situations as calling for ethical response (Verbeek 2011). Navigation apps that route users through "efficient" paths are also making moral choices about which neighborhoods receive foot traffic, which businesses receive patronage, which communities receive the economic benefits of urban mobility. An ethics of algorithmic wandering must engage with this moral-mediating dimension of navigation technology, attending not only to the experiential but also to the distributive consequences of algorithmic routing.

The philosophical framework most appropriate for this ethics is, I suggest, *relational autonomy* -- the understanding, developed by Mackenzie and Stoljar (2000) and others, that autonomy is not an individual property but a capacity that is constituted, sustained, or undermined by the social and technological relations within which agents are embedded. On this account, the question is not whether the individual walker is "free" from algorithmic influence (she never is, just as she was never free from the influence of urban planning, social norms, or commercial advertising) but whether the *relational conditions* of her spatial agency support or undermine her capacity for self-directed engagement with the urban environment.

This relational framework has several advantages. It avoids the naive individualism of the neo-Luddite position (which imagines autonomy as the absence of technological mediation). It avoids the technological determinism of the strong "collapse of serendipity" thesis (which treats algorithmic mediation as an irresistible force). And it provides a basis for evaluating specific technologies, designs, and policies in terms of their effects on the relational conditions of spatial autonomy.

### 4.3 Between Individual Tactics and Structural Reform

The Situationist tradition suggests practices of algorithmic resistance -- deliberate techniques for disrupting the algorithmic determination of spatial experience. Such practices might include: deliberately ignoring navigation suggestions and allowing oneself to become lost; seeking out the algorithmically invisible -- the unreviewed restaurant, the untagged neighborhood; cultivating open receptivity by periodically disabling notifications, closing apps, and attending to the ambient textures of the urban environment.

But we must be honest about the limitations of individual tactics. The Situationists themselves debated this tension: Debord worried that the derive could become a mere lifestyle accessory, a form of bohemian leisure that left the structures of the spectacle intact. The same danger applies to "practices of algorithmic resistance." If the underlying power structures -- the platform companies' business models, the data extraction regimes, the attention-capturing design patterns -- remain unchanged, individual acts of refusal may amount to little more than what Thomas Frank (1997) called "the conquest of cool" -- the commodification of resistance itself.

An adequate ethics of algorithmic wandering must therefore operate at multiple levels:

**At the level of individual practice**, it proposes the cultivation of what we might call *critical spatial literacy* -- the capacity to recognize, reflect on, and selectively resist the algorithmic structuring of one's spatial experience. This is not a matter of rejecting technology but of developing a reflective relationship to it -- what Verbeek calls "accompanying technology" (Verbeek 2005, 235).

**At the level of design**, it calls for navigation and recommendation systems that incorporate principles of spatial autonomy: offering multiple routes with different experiential qualities rather than a single "optimal" path; surfacing unexpected and locally significant destinations alongside algorithmically predicted ones; designing interfaces that support environmental engagement rather than screen dependence. The emerging field of "calm technology" (Case 2015) and the concept of "serendipity engines" offer concrete design resources.

**At the level of policy and governance**, it calls for regulatory frameworks that address the spatial power of platform companies -- including requirements for algorithmic transparency in navigation and recommendation systems, protections against discriminatory algorithmic routing, and public investment in non-commercial digital infrastructure for urban navigation. Kitchin and Dodge's (2011) concept of "code/space" -- the mutual constitution of software and spatial experience -- provides a theoretical foundation for understanding why the governance of algorithmic systems is inseparable from the governance of urban space.

### 4.4 Toward a Right to Spatial Self-Determination

Finally, the analysis suggests the need for a political concept that addresses the algorithmic structuring of spatial experience. Building on Williams's concept of "freedom of attention" (Williams 2018), Floridi's framework of informational self-determination (Floridi 2013), and Hildebrandt's right to not be profiled (Hildebrandt 2015), I propose the concept of a *right to spatial self-determination* -- the right of the urban subject to move through space under conditions that support, rather than undermine, the capacity for autonomous spatial agency.

This right has both negative and positive dimensions. Negatively, it protects against surveillance, profiling, and algorithmic manipulation of spatial behavior -- the right to walk through a city without one's movement being tracked, predicted, and nudged by commercial algorithms. Positively, it affirms the value of spatial experiences that resist optimization -- the detour, the digression, the aimless wander, the chance encounter -- and calls for the creation of material and technological conditions that support such experiences.

The concept of spatial self-determination is grounded in the relational autonomy framework developed above. It recognizes that spatial autonomy is not the absence of mediation but a quality of the mediating relations through which spatial experience is constituted. A navigation app that offers multiple routes with different experiential qualities supports spatial self-determination; an app that provides only an "optimal" route and tracks the user's compliance with it undermines it. The right to spatial self-determination is thus not a right to unmediated experience (which is impossible) but a right to *mediating conditions* that support autonomous spatial agency.

---

## Conclusion

The figure of the flaneur has haunted urban theory for over a century because it crystallizes a question that modernity has never resolved: what does it mean to inhabit a city *freely*? Benjamin recognized that this freedom was already under siege in the nineteenth century. Debord and the Situationists diagnosed a further intensification. De Certeau found resources for resistance in the everyday tactical improvisations of ordinary walkers.

The age of algorithms represents a new phase of this ongoing struggle. The algorithmic flaneur -- the smartphone-carrying urban subject whose path, perception, and encounter are increasingly shaped by AI systems -- inhabits a city that is, in important respects, different from anything that Benjamin, Debord, or de Certeau could have envisioned. The mediation of urban experience is no longer accomplished primarily through built architecture or commodity display but through data flows, recommendation algorithms, and attention-capturing interfaces -- all sustained by a vast material infrastructure of servers, cables, and satellites that is itself a form of urban architecture, largely invisible but constitutive.

Yet the philosophical resources developed by these thinkers remain indispensable. The flaneur's commitment to attentive perception, the derive's cultivation of serendipitous encounter, the tactical walker's creative subversion of administered space -- these practices and their underlying values provide the basis for an ethics of algorithmic wandering. That ethics must be, as this essay has argued, multi-leveled -- operating at the scales of individual practice, technological design, and political governance. It must be attentive to difference -- recognizing that algorithmic mediation affects urban subjects differently depending on their embodied, social, and political circumstances. And it must be grounded in a relational understanding of autonomy that recognizes the inevitability of technological mediation while insisting on the possibility of shaping that mediation toward spatial freedom.

The challenge is not to return to a mythical pre-technological city -- such a city never existed -- but to develop practices, designs, and political frameworks that preserve the porosity of urban experience: the city's capacity to surprise us, challenge us, and reveal possibilities that no algorithm could predict. This requires recognizing that the material infrastructure of algorithmic urbanism -- the server farms, the fiber-optic networks, the smartphone supply chains -- is as much a part of urban architecture as the streets and buildings through which we walk. And it requires insisting, against the logic of optimization, that the measure of a good city is not the efficiency with which it transports us from destination to destination but the richness and openness of the experience it offers to all who move through it.

The question of the algorithmic flaneur is, in the end, the question of what kind of urban subjects we wish to become -- and what kind of cities we wish to build to become them. It is a question that demands not only philosophical reflection but practical engagement with the material, technological, and political conditions of urban life. The flaneur's leisurely gaze, the deriviste's political wandering, the tactical walker's creative subversion -- these remain resources for imagining and enacting a different relationship to the algorithmic city. The task is to translate them into forms of practice, design, and governance adequate to the present moment.
