## Reviewer 1: Peter-Paul Verbeek

**Affiliation**: University of Twente, Department of Philosophy
**Expertise**: Postphenomenology, Philosophy of Technology, Technological Mediation Theory

### Scores

| Criterion | Score (1-10) | Comment |
|-----------|:---:|---------|
| Originality | 7 | The synthesis of psychogeographic tradition with postphenomenology and attention economics is genuinely novel, though the individual theoretical components are well-established. |
| Theoretical Framework | 6 | Draws productively on multiple traditions but sometimes conflates distinct phenomenological vocabularies without sufficient justification. The use of my own mediation theory is generally accurate but occasionally oversimplified. |
| Methodology | 5 | As a philosophical essay, the methodology is primarily genealogical and conceptual. However, the paper lacks any empirical grounding or engagement with empirical studies of smartphone-mediated navigation behavior. |
| Literature Engagement | 7 | Strong coverage of the psychogeographic tradition and competent engagement with postphenomenology, though some significant omissions (see below). |
| Argumentation & Evidence | 6 | The central argument is compelling in its broad outlines but insufficiently differentiated in its analysis of specific technological mediations. |
| Clarity & Writing | 8 | Exceptionally well-written, with a lucid prose style that makes complex philosophical concepts accessible without sacrificing rigor. |
| Significance | 7 | Makes a meaningful contribution to the intersection of philosophy of technology and urban theory. |

**Overall Score**: 6.6 / 10

### Strengths

1. **Productive theoretical synthesis.** The paper succeeds in bringing together three intellectual traditions -- psychogeography, postphenomenology, and attention economics -- that have remained largely separate in the literature. The concept of the "algorithmic flaneur" is an evocative and analytically useful figure for exploring their intersection.

2. **Excellent genealogical structure.** The progression from Benjamin through Debord and de Certeau to the contemporary algorithmic landscape is deftly handled. The author demonstrates genuine command of the psychogeographic tradition and avoids the common error of treating these thinkers as a monolithic school.

3. **The concept of "attentional architecture" is genuinely illuminating.** The argument that attention-capturing interfaces function as a new layer of spatial architecture, superimposed upon the physical city, is a significant conceptual contribution that deserves further development.

4. **Writing quality.** The prose is consistently elegant, precise, and engaging -- a rare combination in contemporary philosophy of technology.

### Weaknesses

1. **Oversimplification of mediation theory.** The paper draws on my framework of human-technology relations but tends to reduce the complexity of technological mediation to a binary opposition between "authentic" embodied experience and "inauthentic" algorithmic determination. This is precisely the kind of essentialism that postphenomenology was developed to overcome. The paper acknowledges (Section 4.2) that simple technological refusal is philosophically naive, but the overall argumentative thrust nonetheless implies a normative hierarchy in which unmediated spatial experience is superior to algorithmically mediated experience. A more rigorous application of mediation theory would recognize that algorithmic navigation constitutes a *new form* of spatial experience, not simply a degraded version of flanerie.

2. **Insufficient differentiation of human-technology relations.** The paper invokes my distinction between embodiment, hermeneutic, alterity, and background relations (Section 3.1) but applies it only to Google Maps. A more thorough analysis would examine how each of the three AI systems discussed (navigation, recommendation, social media) establishes different types of mediation relations. Instagram's mediation of urban visuality, for instance, involves composite intentionality in ways that differ fundamentally from Google Maps' hermeneutic mediation.

3. **Neglect of the "moral mediation" dimension.** My more recent work on moral mediation and "designing for morality" is entirely absent from the analysis. If the paper wishes to develop an ethics of algorithmic wandering (Section 4), it must engage with the growing literature on how technologies actively shape moral perception and decision-making, not merely perceptual experience.

4. **The concept of "double enframing" is underdeveloped.** The paper introduces this intriguing concept in Section 2.3 but does not adequately distinguish it from standard Heideggerian enframing or justify why a new concept is needed. Moreover, the relationship between Heideggerian and postphenomenological frameworks is left unresolved -- a significant tension given the well-documented methodological differences between classical phenomenology and postphenomenology.

### Detailed Comments

- Section 1.4 claims that algorithmic mediation "threatens to close" the porosity of the perceptual field. This is too strong. Empirical research on GPS navigation (e.g., Leshed et al. 2008; Speake 2015) suggests more nuanced effects -- some users report *enhanced* spatial awareness through digital mapping, while others report diminished engagement. The paper needs to engage with this complexity rather than assuming a unidirectional phenomenological transformation.

- The "right to algorithmic opacity" proposed in Section 4.4 is provocative but insufficiently developed. How does it relate to existing frameworks for informational self-determination (Floridi), the right to not be profiled (Hildebrandt), or my own proposals for "mediation design"? The paper introduces a significant ethical concept without adequate philosophical elaboration.

- The discussion of Zuboff's surveillance capitalism (Section 3.4) is overly reliant on Zuboff's own framework without engaging with the substantial critical literature that has questioned her account (e.g., Morozov 2019; Sadowski 2020). This weakens the political-economic dimension of the argument.

### Suggestions for Revision

1. Develop a more nuanced account of how different AI systems establish different types of mediation relations, using the full postphenomenological toolkit.
2. Engage with empirical research on smartphone-mediated navigation to qualify the paper's strong claims about phenomenological transformation.
3. Elaborate the "right to algorithmic opacity" in dialogue with existing philosophical frameworks for informational rights.
4. Address the tension between Heideggerian and postphenomenological approaches rather than treating them as seamlessly compatible.
5. Incorporate the moral mediation literature into the ethics section.

### Decision: **Major Revision**

The paper has significant potential but requires substantial theoretical refinement to deliver on its ambitious promises. The core concept is strong, but the execution needs more rigor in its application of postphenomenological categories and more engagement with counterarguments and empirical evidence.

## Reviewer 2: Shannon Mattern

**Affiliation**: University of Pennsylvania, Department of History and Sociology of Science
**Expertise**: Urban Media, Infrastructure Studies, Media Archaeology, Critical Data Studies

### Scores

| Criterion | Score (1-10) | Comment |
|-----------|:---:|---------|
| Originality | 7 | The synthesis is novel, though the individual arguments about algorithmic mediation of urban space are increasingly common in critical urban studies. |
| Theoretical Framework | 7 | Well-constructed philosophical framework, though it would benefit from engagement with media archaeology and infrastructure studies. |
| Methodology | 4 | The paper operates at a high level of abstraction without grounding its claims in specific urban contexts, material infrastructures, or empirical observation. |
| Literature Engagement | 6 | Strong in phenomenology and psychogeography but thin in its engagement with urban studies, media studies, and STS literatures. |
| Argumentation & Evidence | 6 | Philosophically rigorous in its own terms but insufficiently attentive to the material, economic, and infrastructural dimensions of algorithmic urbanism. |
| Clarity & Writing | 9 | Outstanding prose quality. The paper is a genuine pleasure to read. |
| Significance | 7 | Would make a meaningful contribution to interdisciplinary dialogue between philosophy and urban studies. |

**Overall Score**: 6.6 / 10

### Strengths

1. **Elegant conceptual architecture.** The paper builds its argument with impressive structural clarity, moving from historical genealogy through phenomenological diagnosis to ethical proposal. The four-part structure mirrors the intellectual progression it traces from Benjamin to the present.

2. **Productive use of my own work.** The citation of *A City Is Not a Computer* (Section 3.3) is apt and well-integrated. The paper's argument about Instagram's pre-curation of the visual field complements my critique of computational reductionism in urban discourse.

3. **The concept of "aesthetic gentrification" is valuable.** The argument that Instagram's visual economy reshapes the physical economy of urban space (Section 3.3) is a compact and powerful formulation that deserves wider circulation.

4. **Effective defamiliarization.** The opening vignette of the traveller arriving in an unfamiliar city succeeds in making visible the usually invisible algorithmic mediation of everyday urban experience.

### Weaknesses

1. **Abstraction from material infrastructure.** The paper treats algorithmic systems as if they were purely immaterial forces shaping urban experience from some ethereal plane. In reality, these systems depend on massive material infrastructures -- server farms, fiber-optic cables, cell towers, satellite networks, smartphone supply chains -- that have their own spatial politics. The paper's phenomenological framework would be enriched by attention to the *materiality* of algorithmic mediation. As I have argued, urban intelligence has always been embedded in physical media -- from clay tablets to fiber optics. The paper's exclusive focus on the experiential dimension of algorithmic mediation risks reproducing the very immaterialist ideology it critiques.

2. **Eurocentrism and universalizing tendencies.** The genealogy from Benjamin to Debord to de Certeau is resolutely European, and the paper's examples (Google Maps, Yelp, Instagram) presume a North American/European urban context. How do these dynamics play out in cities where different navigation apps dominate (Baidu Maps in China, Yandex in Russia), where informal urban economies resist algorithmic capture, or where smartphone penetration is uneven? The paper's universalizing claims about "urban experience" need qualification.

3. **Insufficient engagement with critical urban studies.** The paper does not engage with relevant work in geography and urban studies on algorithmic urbanism, platform urbanism, and data-driven cities. While it cites Caprotti et al. (2022) and Cugurullo et al. (2024) in the references, these sources are not substantively discussed. Work by Ash Amin, Nigel Thrift, Rob Kitchin, and others on algorithmic spatiality would significantly enrich the analysis.

4. **Gender and the flaneur.** The paper briefly acknowledges (Section 3.2) that the flaneur's "freedom" was a privilege of class and gender, but this acknowledgment is perfunctory. The feminist critique of the flaneur (Wolff 1985; Parsons 2000; D'Souza and McDonough 2006) has demonstrated that the figure is not simply gendered but constitutively exclusionary. An updated "algorithmic flaneur" must reckon with the ways in which algorithmic systems reproduce and amplify existing inequalities of race, class, gender, and disability in urban space. Algorithmic bias in navigation and recommendation systems does not affect all urban subjects equally.

5. **Romanticization of pre-algorithmic urbanism.** Despite the paper's disclaimer (Section 4.2) that it does not advocate for a return to a "mythical pre-technological city," its rhetorical structure consistently frames algorithmic mediation as a *loss* -- of serendipity, openness, freedom. This implicitly romanticizes pre-digital urban experience and underestimates the ways in which algorithmic tools can enhance urban navigation for people with disabilities, non-native speakers, or those unfamiliar with local geographies.

### Detailed Comments

- The paper's treatment of Google Maps (Section 3.1) overlooks the fact that Google Maps has undergone significant evolution. Recent versions incorporate features like "vibe" search, AR walking directions, and immersive views that complicate the paper's characterization of the app as purely optimizing efficiency. The analysis needs to engage with specific, current versions of these technologies rather than treating them as static.

- The "collapse of serendipity" thesis (Section 3.4) would benefit from engagement with the empirical literature on serendipity in information science (e.g., Foster and Ford 2003; McCay-Peet and Toms 2015) and recent work on "serendipity engines" -- systems designed to promote unexpected encounters.

- The paper's bibliography, while philosophically strong, is notably thin in urban studies, geography, and STS. Important works by Rob Kitchin (*The Data Revolution*, *Data-Driven Urbanism*), Ash Amin and Nigel Thrift (*Cities: Reimagining the Urban*), and Sarah Elwood and Agnieszka Leszczynski on digital geographies are absent.

### Suggestions for Revision

1. Add a section or substantial discussion addressing the material infrastructure underlying algorithmic urbanism -- server farms, network architectures, device supply chains.
2. Qualify universalizing claims by acknowledging non-Western and non-metropolitan urban contexts.
3. Engage substantively with the feminist critique of the flaneur and with algorithmic bias in urban technologies.
4. Incorporate relevant scholarship from geography, STS, and critical data studies.
5. Address the ways in which algorithmic tools can *enhance* rather than only *diminish* urban experience, particularly for marginalized populations.

### Decision: **Major Revision**

The paper is beautifully written and conceptually ambitious, but it needs to be brought into dialogue with the broader interdisciplinary literature on algorithmic urbanism and qualified in its claims about universality and loss. With substantial revision, it could make an important contribution.

## Reviewer 3: James Williams

**Affiliation**: University of Oxford, Faculty of Philosophy
**Expertise**: Attention Economy, Technology Ethics, Digital Freedom, Philosophy of Action

### Scores

| Criterion | Score (1-10) | Comment |
|-----------|:---:|---------|
| Originality | 8 | The spatial turn in attention economy analysis is genuinely novel and overdue. The paper opens a productive new line of inquiry. |
| Theoretical Framework | 7 | Strong integration of phenomenological, psychogeographic, and attention-economic frameworks, though some tensions remain unresolved. |
| Methodology | 6 | The conceptual methodology is sound for a philosophical essay, though the paper would benefit from engagement with empirical evidence and case studies. |
| Literature Engagement | 7 | Thorough engagement with the primary theoretical sources, though some important recent work is missing. |
| Argumentation & Evidence | 7 | The argument builds effectively but some key transitions need strengthening. |
| Clarity & Writing | 9 | Superb writing. Among the best-written philosophy papers I have reviewed in recent years. |
| Significance | 8 | Opens an important new area of inquiry at the intersection of attention ethics and urban phenomenology. |

**Overall Score**: 7.4 / 10

### Strengths

1. **The spatial dimension of the attention economy.** This is the paper's single most important contribution. The argument that the attention economy operates *spatially* -- restructuring not just how long we attend but *where* and *to what* in physical space -- is a significant extension of the attention economy framework that I and others have developed. Section 2.1's claim that "the smartphone does not merely compete with the urban environment for the walker's attention; it overlays itself upon the environment" is exactly right, and the implications deserve sustained exploration.

2. **Effective use of my tripartite distraction framework.** The paper accurately presents my distinction between functional, existential, and epistemic distraction (Section 2.1) and productively extends it into the spatial domain. The suggestion that algorithmic navigation produces all three forms of distraction simultaneously -- functional (diverting attention from the physical environment), existential (undermining the capacity for self-directed exploration), and epistemic (reducing the walker's capacity for spatial reasoning) -- is a valuable contribution.

3. **The "collapse of serendipity" as an ethical concept.** The paper's framing of serendipity as a phenomenological category with ethical significance (Section 3.4) is philosophically rich and practically important. The distinction between social constraints on encounter (which can be tactically subverted) and algorithmic constraints (which operate by preemption) is particularly incisive.

4. **Compelling genealogical narrative.** The paper constructs a persuasive intellectual lineage from Benjamin through Debord and de Certeau that illuminates the contemporary situation without being merely antiquarian.

### Weaknesses

1. **Insufficient engagement with agency and autonomy.** The paper's ethical framework (Section 4) would benefit from more sustained engagement with the philosophical literature on autonomy, particularly relational autonomy (Mackenzie and Stoljar 2000) and the conditions for autonomous action in technologically mediated environments. The "freedom of attention" that I propose is not merely a freedom *from* distraction but a positive capacity for self-directed engagement with the world. The paper's emphasis on algorithmic determination sometimes implies a passive subject who is simply "shaped" by technology, rather than an agent who actively negotiates the terms of technological mediation.

2. **The "right to algorithmic opacity" needs philosophical grounding.** I find this concept (Section 4.4) intriguing but underdeveloped. The paper asserts this right without providing a philosophical justification for it. Is it grounded in autonomy? Dignity? Privacy? What is its relationship to existing rights frameworks? Moreover, "opacity" is a potentially misleading term -- it suggests that the solution is to make oneself *invisible* to algorithms, when the deeper issue is the *structure* of algorithmic authority over spatial experience. A "right to algorithmic self-determination" or "right to unoptimized space" might better capture the paper's intent.

3. **Undertheorized relationship between individual and structural resistance.** Section 4.3 proposes "practices of algorithmic resistance" (deliberately getting lost, seeking out unreviewed restaurants, etc.) that are presented as "political gestures." But the paper does not adequately address the tension between individual resistance and structural change. Are these practices genuinely political, or are they lifestyle choices that leave the underlying power structures intact? The Situationists themselves debated this tension extensively, and the paper should engage with it more directly.

4. **Missing engagement with recent attention economy literature.** The paper cites my 2018 book but does not engage with more recent work on the attention economy, including Nir Eyal's constructive proposals for "indistractable" technology, Tim Wu's *The Attention Merchants* (2016) on the history of attention capture, or Yves Citton's *The Ecology of Attention* (2017) on collective and environmental dimensions of attention. Citton's work, in particular, on "attention ecologies" would complement the paper's spatial analysis.

### Detailed Comments

- The paper's opening vignette is effective but slightly cliched. The "traveller arriving in an unfamiliar city" scenario has been used many times in this literature. A more specific, concrete example might be more compelling.

- Section 2.3's discussion of "double enframing" is intriguing but risks obscuring the distinctiveness of the paper's own contribution by tethering it too closely to Heidegger's framework. The paper's analysis of how attention is *divided* between screen and street is more original and more phenomenologically precise than any Heideggerian vocabulary can capture. I would encourage the author to develop this analysis in its own terms rather than forcing it into Heideggerian categories.

- The conclusion is effective but could be strengthened by offering a more concrete vision of what "algorithmic wandering" might look like in practice. The paper proposes an ethics but stops short of imagining specific designs, policies, or practices that would instantiate it.

### Suggestions for Revision

1. Ground the "right to algorithmic opacity" (or a renamed concept) in a specific philosophical framework for rights and autonomy.
2. Engage more substantially with the tension between individual resistance and structural change in Section 4.3.
3. Incorporate recent work on attention ecologies (Citton) and the history of attention capture (Wu).
4. Develop the analysis of divided attention in its own phenomenological terms rather than relying on the Heideggerian framework.
5. Provide more concrete examples and, where possible, engagement with empirical research on algorithmic navigation behavior.

### Decision: **Minor Revision**

This is a strong and important paper that opens a genuinely new line of philosophical inquiry. The core argument is sound and significant. The revisions needed are matters of refinement, grounding, and engagement with additional literature rather than fundamental reconceptualization. I recommend acceptance after minor revision.

## Editor Synthesis

**Editor**: Dr. Mark Coeckelbergh
**Journal**: *Philosophy & Technology*
**Date**: March 9, 2026

### Meta-Review

The manuscript "The Algorithmic Flaneur" has been reviewed by three expert referees with complementary expertise in postphenomenology and technological mediation (Reviewer 1), urban media and infrastructure studies (Reviewer 2), and attention economy ethics (Reviewer 3). All three reviewers recognize the paper's exceptional prose quality, its novel synthetic ambition, and the potential significance of its core concepts ("algorithmic flaneur," "attentional architecture," "collapse of serendipity"). Reviewer 3 is the most enthusiastic, recommending minor revision; Reviewers 1 and 2 recommend major revision.

### Key Strengths Identified Across Reviews

1. **Conceptual originality**: All reviewers agree that the synthesis of psychogeographic tradition with postphenomenology and attention economics is genuinely novel and opens a productive new line of inquiry.
2. **Writing quality**: All three reviewers independently praise the exceptional quality of the prose (scores of 8, 9, and 9).
3. **Structural clarity**: The genealogical architecture of the paper is recognized as effective and well-executed.
4. **Specific conceptual contributions**: The concepts of "attentional architecture," "aesthetic gentrification," and the "collapse of serendipity" are identified as valuable contributions.

### Critical Issues Requiring Attention

1. **Theoretical oversimplification (Reviewers 1 and 2)**: The paper's application of postphenomenological categories needs greater nuance. Reviewer 1 specifically identifies an implicit normative hierarchy between unmediated and mediated experience that contradicts postphenomenological principles. The paper must differentiate more carefully between the types of mediation relations established by different technologies.

2. **Lack of empirical grounding (all three reviewers)**: All reviewers note the absence of engagement with empirical research on smartphone-mediated navigation and urban experience. While the paper is primarily philosophical, its claims about phenomenological transformation need to be tested against empirical evidence.

3. **Insufficient engagement with broader literatures (Reviewers 2 and 3)**: The paper needs to engage with (a) critical urban studies, geography, and STS literatures on algorithmic urbanism; (b) feminist critiques of the flaneur; (c) recent attention economy scholarship; and (d) the moral mediation literature.

4. **Material and political blind spots (Reviewer 2)**: The paper's exclusive focus on the experiential dimension of algorithmic mediation neglects material infrastructure, non-Western urban contexts, and the ways in which algorithmic tools can enhance urban experience for marginalized populations.

5. **Underdeveloped ethical framework (Reviewers 1 and 3)**: The "right to algorithmic opacity" and the "practices of algorithmic resistance" need more rigorous philosophical grounding. The paper must address the tension between individual and structural resistance and engage with existing frameworks for informational rights and autonomous agency.

### Revision Instructions

The author is invited to submit a revised manuscript addressing the following requirements:

**Required Revisions:**

1. **Refine the postphenomenological analysis**: Differentiate the types of mediation relations established by navigation apps, recommendation platforms, and social media respectively. Avoid implying a normative hierarchy between unmediated and mediated experience. Engage with Verbeek's moral mediation framework.

2. **Incorporate empirical evidence**: Engage with at least some empirical research on smartphone-mediated navigation and urban experience to ground and qualify the paper's phenomenological claims.

3. **Address material and contextual dimensions**: Acknowledge the material infrastructure underlying algorithmic urbanism and qualify universalizing claims by noting non-Western and non-metropolitan contexts.

4. **Engage with feminist critique**: Address the gendered, racialized, and classed dimensions of both the historical flaneur and the algorithmic flaneur. Discuss how algorithmic bias differentially affects urban subjects.

5. **Strengthen the ethical framework**: Ground the proposed rights concept in established philosophical frameworks for autonomy and rights. Address the tension between individual resistance and structural change. Engage with relational autonomy and attention ecology literatures.

6. **Expand literature engagement**: Incorporate Citton (attention ecologies), Wu (attention history), Kitchin (data-driven urbanism), and feminist flaneur criticism. Engage critically with the secondary literature on surveillance capitalism (Morozov, Sadowski).

**Recommended (but not required) Revisions:**

7. Replace or supplement the opening vignette with more specific, concrete examples.
8. Develop the "double enframing" concept more fully or consider reframing the analysis in the paper's own phenomenological terms.
9. Consider the ways in which algorithmic tools can enhance urban experience for people with disabilities, non-native speakers, and others.

### Decision: **Major Revision**

The manuscript demonstrates exceptional intellectual ambition and writing quality. Its core concepts have the potential to make a significant contribution to philosophy of technology and urban theory. However, the theoretical, empirical, and political gaps identified by the reviewers must be addressed before the paper can be accepted. I look forward to receiving the revised manuscript.
