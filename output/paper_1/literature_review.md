# Literature Review: LLMs, Collective Memory, and Historical Narratives in the Digital Public Sphere

## Overview
This literature review synthesizes 20 published works spanning digital memory studies, AI and public discourse, algorithmic mediation of historical narratives, and cultural bias in large language models. Sources were identified through systematic searches of academic databases and repositories.

---

## 1. Yasseri, T. (2025). "The Memory Machine: How Large Language Models Shape Our Collective Past." *Verfassungsblog*.
**Summary**: Argues that LLMs pose risks to the diversity and plurality of collective memory by standardizing shared narratives. Proposes regulatory frameworks, digital literacy initiatives, and ethical AI development to prevent homogenization of historical understanding. Connects standardization of collective memory to AI-mediated information processes.

## 2. Hoskins, A. (2024). "AI and Memory." *Memory, Mind & Media*, Cambridge University Press.
**Summary**: Examines how the advent of ChatGPT and rapid AI development creates a "new battleground between humans and computers in the shaping of reality." Argues that AI platforms blur boundaries between implicit/explicit, unconscious/conscious, individual/collective memory. Discusses ChatGPT's 2024 rollout of "memory" features as a personalization of surveillance.

## 3. Gensburger, S. & Clavert, F. (2024). "AI As Artificial Memory: A Global Reconfiguration of Our Collective Memory Practices?" *Memory Studies Review*, 1(2), 231ff. Brill.
**Summary**: Conceptualizes the impact of AI tools on collective memory, noting the challenge of defining collective memory given "the multitude of competing approaches since Halbwachs." Argues that AI models aggregate massive data about the past in synthetic fashion, forming distributed memories that evolve based on user interactions. Part of a special issue asking whether AI is the future of collective memory.

## 4. Pesta, B. et al. (2024). "Large Language Models Based on Historical Text Could Offer Informative Tools for Behavioral Science." *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS)*.
**Summary**: Explores training LLMs on historical texts to create Historical LLMs (HLLMs) that contain traces of the collective mentality of past populations. Proposes using HLLMs to simulate historical societies' responses on modern psychological instruments, drawing on fiction, plays, diaries, letters, and scholarly texts.

## 5. Adriaansen, R. & Smit, A. (2025). "Collective Memory and Social Media." *Current Opinion in Psychology*, ScienceDirect.
**Summary**: Reviews how platformization reshapes remembering and forgetting through algorithmic curation. Identifies new mnemonic practices: hashtag commemoration, memetic memory, and digital memory activism. Highlights AI as "emerging memory agents" and identifies challenges including Western-centric bias and data access limitations.

## 6. Keightley, E. & Schlesinger, P. (2024). "The Multiplicities of Platformed Remembering." *Memory, Mind & Media*, Cambridge University Press.
**Summary**: Examines how platformization—"the penetration of economic, governmental, and infrastructural extensions of digital platforms"—colonizes personal and collective pasts for commercial benefit. Argues platforms are not neutral intermediaries in memory construction, whether through YouTube for cultural memory or diary apps for autobiographical memory.

## 7. Makhortykh, M. (2023). "The User is Dead, Long Live the Platform? Problematising the User-Centric Focus of (Digital) Memory Studies." *Memory Studies*, SAGE.
**Summary**: Critiques the user-centric focus of digital memory studies, arguing that commercial recommender systems influence remembrance through logics focused on maximizing profits. Contends that contemporary memory infrastructures limit capabilities for forgetting, diminishing human agency over digital memory traces.

## 8. Smit, A. (2024). "Memory in the Digital Age." *Open Research Europe / PMC*.
**Summary**: Contrasts Halbwachs' notion of the social with Gabriel Tarde's alternative proposal, arguing the latter enables refinement of the concept of "collective" in digital memory. Maintains that despite digital transformations, changes do not imply a new ontology of memory but rather a materialization of theoretical claims made by Memory Studies since its inception.

## 9. Sartori, L. & Theodorou, A. (2022). "A Sociotechnical Perspective for the Future of AI: Narratives, Inequalities, and Human Control." *Ethics and Information Technology*, 24, 4. Springer.
**Summary**: Examines AI through a sociotechnical lens, highlighting how narratives about AI shape public perception and policy. Discusses inequalities embedded in AI systems and the importance of maintaining human control over algorithmic decision-making.

## 10. Argyle, L. et al. (2023). "Leveraging AI for Democratic Discourse: Chat Interventions Can Improve Online Political Conversations at Scale." *PNAS*.
**Summary**: Develops an AI chat assistant making real-time, evidence-based suggestions in divisive political conversations. In a randomized controlled trial, the assistant increased conversation quality and willingness to grant opponents space to express views, suggesting AI can improve rather than degrade discourse.

## 11. Seger, E. et al. (2024). "Don't Panic (Yet): Assessing the Evidence and Discourse Around Generative AI and Elections." Knight First Amendment Institute, Columbia University.
**Summary**: Critically examines claims about AI-driven electoral manipulation during the 2023-2024 global election cycle. Argues that despite predictions of deepfake-driven manipulation, AI's influence on elections was overshadowed by other factors. Provides nuanced assessment of generative AI's actual versus perceived impact on democratic discourse.

## 12. Oreja-Guevara, J. & Bernhard, U. (2024). "Does Algorithmic Content Moderation Promote Democratic Discourse? Radical Democratic Critique of Toxic Language AI." *Information, Communication & Society*, Taylor & Francis.
**Summary**: Provides a normative critique of civility assumptions embedded in algorithmic moderation (e.g., Google's Perspective API). Distinguishes between incivility and intolerance from a radical democratic standpoint, recognizing potential political and symbolic values of incivility for marginalized groups.

## 13. Bruns, S. et al. (2025). "Generative AI and Misinformation: A Scoping Review." *AI & Society*, Springer.
**Summary**: Synthesizes 24 empirical studies on the dual role of generative AI in misinformation generation, detection, and mitigation. Finds LLMs can generate highly convincing misinformation exploiting cognitive biases, while also demonstrating ability to detect false claims. Positions LLMs as "influential agents in shaping public discourse and knowledge production."

## 14. Mackay, H. & Chia, A. (2024). "Who Speaks Through the Machine? Generative AI as Discourse and Implications for Management." *Scandinavian Journal of Management*, ScienceDirect.
**Summary**: Draws on Foucauldian "discursive formation" to conceptualize generative AI's influence, identifying a dual discourse: one from technological solutionism and another from ChatGPT's utterances shaped by algorithmic, epistemic, and linguistic influences. Examines how AI-mediated discourse affects organizational practices and decision-making.

## 15. Tao, Y. et al. (2025). "A Framework for Evaluating Cultural Bias and Historical Misconceptions in LLM Outputs." *BenchCouncil Transactions on Benchmarks, Standards and Evaluations*, ScienceDirect.
**Summary**: Proposes a framework integrating culturally sensitive prompts, statistical benchmarking, and human-in-the-loop verification for evaluating cultural bias. Introduces the Cultural Bias Score (CBS) and Historical Misconception Score (HMS). Finds training data disproportionately reflects Western-centric perspectives, amplifying dominant narratives while marginalizing underrepresented viewpoints.

## 16. Tao, R. et al. (2024). "Cultural Bias and Cultural Alignment of Large Language Models." *PNAS Nexus*, 3(9), Oxford Academic.
**Summary**: Conducts a disaggregated evaluation of cultural bias across 107 countries for five widely used LLMs. Finds LLMs trained on predominantly English text exhibit latent bias favoring Western cultural values, especially when prompted in English. Provides empirical evidence for systematic cultural hegemony in LLM outputs.

## 17. Navigli, R. et al. (2023). "Biases in Large Language Models: Origins, Inventory, and Discussion." *Journal of Data and Information Quality*, ACM.
**Summary**: Surveys types of social bias in LLM-generated text, ranging from gender to age, sexual orientation to ethnicity, religion to culture. Provides a comprehensive inventory of bias types and discusses their origins in training data and model architecture.

## 18. Hutson, J. & Plate, L. (2025). "Handling the Hype: Demystifying Artificial Intelligence for Memory Studies." Landecker Digital Memory Lab / *Memory, Mind & Media*.
**Summary**: Uses "chatbots" as shorthand for genAI and argues that "all AI systems sit within wider social, political, cultural and environmental assemblages." Highlights that chatbots work discriminatorily by silencing or misrepresenting minority groups and that training dataset size does not guarantee diversity.

## 19. Hintz, A. et al. (2025). "Unsettling Memories: Practice, Platforms, and Possibilities." *Memory, Mind & Media*, Cambridge University Press.
**Summary**: Argues platforms, algorithms, and AI are fundamentally reshaping everyday dynamics of media and memory. Contends forgetting demands renewed scholarly attention—whether through algorithmic omission, platform decay, or deliberate erasure—as an active process shaping what remains knowable and shareable.

## 20. de Vries, K. &"; Sauer, S. (2025). "Tracing the Bias Loop: AI, Cultural Heritage and Bias-Mitigating in Practice." *AI & Society*, Springer.
**Summary**: Investigates bias within AI-driven cultural heritage collections, emphasizing how digital technologies both inherit and amplify existing societal and historical prejudices embedded in analogue records. Examines the "bias loop" wherein historical biases are encoded, amplified, and reproduced through AI systems.

## Thematic Clusters

### A. AI and Collective Memory Theory
Papers 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 engage directly with how AI systems reconfigure collective memory. They draw on Halbwachs and subsequent theorists to examine whether LLMs constitute a fundamentally new form of memory practice or merely materialize existing theoretical insights.

### B. Platformization and Digital Remembering
Papers 5, 6, 7, 19 examine how platform logics—commercial incentives, algorithmic curation, data persistence—reshape what societies remember and forget. They highlight the tension between democratized memory practices and commercial colonization of the past.

### C. AI, Discourse, and the Public Sphere
Papers 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 investigate AI's role in shaping public discourse, from democratic conversations to misinformation to electoral manipulation. They reveal both the potential for AI to improve discourse and the risks of AI-generated misinformation.

### D. Cultural Bias and Historical Representation in LLMs
Papers 4, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20 examine how LLM training data encodes Western-centric biases that shape historical representation, cultural heritage, and the reproduction of dominant narratives at the expense of marginalized perspectives.
