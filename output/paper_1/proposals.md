# Research Proposal: Large Language Models as Cultural Agents — How AI-Generated Text Reshapes Collective Memory and Historical Narratives in the Digital Public Sphere

---

## 1. Thesis Statement

This study argues that large language models (LLMs) function as *cultural agents of memory*—not merely as information retrieval tools, but as active participants in the social construction and transmission of collective memory. Drawing on Maurice Halbwachs's theory of collective memory and its contemporary extensions, the study develops an integrated theoretical framework—the *Algorithmic Memory Framework* (AMF)—to explain how LLMs mediate, reshape, and potentially homogenize historical narratives in the digital public sphere. Through mixed-methods empirical analysis of LLM-generated historical texts, the study demonstrates that these systems introduce a new mode of mnemonic socialization characterized by epistemic flattening, presentism, and Western-centric framing, with significant implications for pluralistic memory cultures.

---

## 2. Research Questions

**RQ1**: How do LLMs construct historical narratives when prompted about contested historical events, and what narrative structures, framing devices, and epistemic authority claims characterize their outputs?

**RQ2**: To what extent do LLM-generated historical narratives tend toward homogenization—flattening legitimate historiographical debate into consensus-driven accounts—and does this tendency vary across platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) and languages (English, Spanish, Mandarin, Arabic)?

**RQ3**: How do users interact with and evaluate LLM-generated historical narratives, and to what extent do these interactions reshape their understanding of the past?

---

## 3. Theoretical Framework

The study integrates three theoretical traditions:

### 3.1 Halbwachs's Collective Memory
Maurice Halbwachs's foundational insight that memory is socially constructed—that individuals remember within "social frameworks" provided by groups, institutions, and cultural artifacts—provides the conceptual backbone. The study extends Halbwachs's framework by treating LLMs as a new type of social framework: algorithmic frameworks of memory that structure how users encounter, interpret, and internalize historical narratives.

### 3.2 Digital Memory Studies
Building on Hoskins (2024), Makhortykh (2023), and Keightley & Schlesinger (2024), the study situates LLMs within the broader platformization of memory. However, it argues that LLMs represent a qualitatively different phenomenon from social media platforms: whereas platforms curate pre-existing user-generated content, LLMs *generate novel memory texts* through statistical synthesis of their training corpora. This distinction requires new theoretical tools.

### 3.3 Critical Algorithm Studies
Drawing on Mackay & Chia's (2024) Foucauldian analysis of generative AI as discourse and Navigli et al.'s (2023) inventory of LLM biases, the study examines how algorithmic processes—training data selection, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), safety filtering—function as *mnemonic gatekeeping mechanisms* that determine which pasts are rendered speakable, which are silenced, and which are distorted.

### 3.4 The Algorithmic Memory Framework (AMF)
The proposed framework identifies four mechanisms through which LLMs reshape collective memory:
1. **Aggregative synthesis**: LLMs compress vast textual corpora into probabilistic models, producing "averaged" accounts of the past that smooth over contradictions and minority perspectives.
2. **Presentist anchoring**: LLMs impose contemporary moral and epistemic frameworks on historical events, reflecting RLHF alignment processes that privilege present-day sensibilities.
3. **Epistemic flattening**: LLMs present historical narratives with uniform confidence, collapsing distinctions between well-established facts, contested interpretations, and speculative claims.
4. **Mnemonic hegemony**: LLMs reproduce the cultural perspectives dominant in their training data (overwhelmingly English-language, Western, and from the Global North), marginalizing alternative memory traditions.

## 4. Methodology

The study employs a sequential mixed-methods design combining computational text analysis with qualitative inquiry.

### Phase 1: Systematic Prompt Experiment (Months 1-4)
- **Sample**: Six contested historical events selected for geographical, temporal, and political diversity (e.g., the Partition of India, the Transatlantic Slave Trade, the Nakba/Israeli Independence, the Rwandan Genocide, the Bombing of Hiroshima, and European colonization of the Americas)
- **Platforms**: Three major LLMs (GPT-4, Claude 3, Gemini Pro)
- **Languages**: English, Spanish, Mandarin, Arabic
- **Protocol**: Standardized prompt battery (n=30 prompts per event) covering factual recall, interpretive framing, perspective-taking, and moral evaluation
- **Analysis**: Computational discourse analysis using topic modeling, sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, and custom-developed measures of narrative diversity and epistemic hedging

### Phase 2: Qualitative Discourse Analysis (Months 4-8)
- **Corpus**: Purposive sample of 200 LLM-generated texts from Phase 1, selected for maximum variation
- **Method**: Critical discourse analysis (CDA) following Fairclough's three-dimensional model, examining textual features, discursive practices, and social practices
- **Focus**: Narrative structures, framing devices, voice and agency attribution, epistemic modality, inclusions/exclusions, and handling of contested interpretations

### Phase 3: User Reception Study (Months 8-12)
- **Sample**: 60 university students across three countries (UK, Mexico, Egypt), recruited through purposive sampling for diversity of historical and cultural background
- **Method**: Semi-structured interviews incorporating think-aloud protocols while participants interact with LLM-generated historical narratives
- **Analysis**: Thematic analysis examining how users evaluate, critique, internalize, or resist LLM-generated accounts of the past

## 5. Expected Contributions

### Theoretical Contributions
- The **Algorithmic Memory Framework (AMF)**, providing the first integrated theoretical model connecting Halbwachs's collective memory theory with the specific mechanisms of LLM text generation
- The concept of **algorithmic mnemonic socialization**, capturing the novel way LLMs introduce users to shared understandings of the past
- Refinement of the concept of **mnemonic hegemony** for the age of generative AI

### Empirical Contributions
- First systematic, cross-platform, cross-linguistic analysis of LLM-generated historical narratives as memory texts
- Empirical evidence on the degree and nature of narrative homogenization across LLM platforms
- Qualitative insights into user reception and co-production of LLM-mediated historical understanding

### Practical Contributions
- Evidence-based recommendations for LLM developers on representing contested histories
- Pedagogical guidelines for educators navigating student use of LLMs for historical inquiry
- Policy recommendations for digital literacy initiatives addressing AI-mediated memory

---

## 7. Significance

As LLMs become primary informational interfaces for hundreds of millions of users, their role in shaping collective memory represents one of the most consequential yet understudied dimensions of the AI revolution. This study provides the theoretical, empirical, and methodological foundations for a new research program at the intersection of memory studies, digital sociology, and AI ethics. By demonstrating how LLMs function as cultural agents that actively reshape historical narratives—rather than neutral conduits of information—the study contributes to urgent public debates about the governance of AI systems and the future of pluralistic democratic memory cultures.
