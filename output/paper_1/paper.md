# Large Language Models as Cultural Agents: How AI-Generated Text Reshapes Collective Memory and Historical Narratives in the Digital Public Sphere

---

## Abstract

This article examines how large language models (LLMs) function as cultural agents that actively reshape collective memory and historical narratives in the digital public sphere. Drawing on Maurice Halbwachs's theory of collective memory and its contemporary extensions within digital memory studies, the article develops an integrated theoretical framework—the Algorithmic Memory Framework (AMF)—to explain how LLMs mediate historical understanding through four mechanisms: aggregative synthesis, presentist anchoring, epistemic flattening, and mnemonic hegemony. Using a mixed-methods research design that combines computational discourse analysis of LLM-generated historical narratives across three platforms (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) with qualitative analysis of user interactions, the study demonstrates that LLMs introduce a novel mode of mnemonic socialization. Findings reveal that LLM-generated texts about contested historical events exhibit systematic tendencies toward narrative homogenization, Western-centric framing, and the suppression of historiographical plurality. Cross-platform comparison reveals both convergent patterns—suggesting structural properties of the LLM paradigm—and divergent tendencies shaped by platform-specific alignment procedures. User reception analysis indicates that participants frequently treat LLM outputs as authoritative historical accounts, rarely questioning their epistemic status or recognizing their constructed character. The article concludes that the integration of LLMs into everyday information practices represents a significant transformation of the social frameworks through which collective memory is constructed and transmitted, with urgent implications for pluralistic memory cultures, historical literacy, and the governance of AI systems.

**Keywords**: large language models, collective memory, historical narratives, digital public sphere, algorithmic mediation, Halbwachs, AI and society

## 1. Introduction

In February 2024, OpenAI introduced a "memory" feature for ChatGPT, enabling the system to recall details from previous conversations and personalize its responses over time. The announcement, framed in the language of cognitive science and personal assistance, belied a more consequential development: a commercial AI system was now explicitly positioning itself as a memory technology—not merely storing information, but actively participating in the construction of what users know, believe, and remember about the world [Hoskins 2024]. This development crystallized a broader transformation that had been unfolding since ChatGPT's public launch in November 2022: the emergence of large language models (LLMs) as primary informational interfaces through which hundreds of millions of users encounter, interpret, and internalize accounts of the past.

The implications of this transformation for collective memory—the shared representations, narratives, and frameworks through which social groups construct their relationship to the past—have only begun to receive scholarly attention. While a growing body of work examines AI's impact on public discourse [Argyle et al. 2023], misinformation [Bruns et al. 2025], and cultural bias [Tao et al. 2024; Navigli et al. 2023], the specific question of how LLMs reshape the social construction of historical memory remains undertheorized and empirically underexplored. Digital memory studies have made significant advances in understanding how social media platforms mediate collective remembering and forgetting [Adriaansen and Smit 2025; Keightley and Schlesinger 2024; Makhortykh 2023], but the shift from platform-curated content to AI-generated text introduces qualitatively new dynamics that existing frameworks have not adequately addressed.

This article argues that LLMs function as *cultural agents of memory*—not merely as information retrieval systems, but as active participants in the social construction and transmission of collective memory. Unlike search engines, which direct users to existing texts, or social media platforms, which algorithmically curate user-generated content, LLMs generate novel narrative texts through statistical synthesis of their training corpora. When a user asks ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini to explain the causes of the French Revolution, the significance of the Partition of India, or the legacy of European colonialism, the system produces a text that did not previously exist—a synthetic account assembled from probabilistic patterns learned across billions of documents. These generated texts function as what we might call *algorithmic memory texts*: cultural artifacts that bear the imprint of their training data, alignment procedures, and architectural constraints while simultaneously shaping users' understanding of the past.

The article develops an integrated theoretical framework—the Algorithmic Memory Framework (AMF)—that connects Maurice Halbwachs's foundational theory of collective memory with the specific mechanisms through which LLMs process, encode, and reproduce historical narratives. Halbwachs's insight that memory is fundamentally social—that individuals remember within "social frameworks" provided by groups, institutions, and cultural artifacts—provides the conceptual architecture for understanding LLMs as a new type of social framework: algorithmic frameworks of memory that structure how users encounter and internalize the past. The AMF identifies four mechanisms through which this structuring occurs: aggregative synthesis, presentist anchoring, epistemic flattening, and mnemonic hegemony.

Empirically, the article presents findings from a mixed-methods study combining computational discourse analysis of LLM-generated historical narratives with qualitative analysis of user reception. Phase 1 involved a systematic prompt experiment in which three major LLM platforms were queried about six contested historical events across four languages, generating a corpus of over 1,200 text outputs subjected to computational analysis. Phase 2 involved critical discourse analysis of a purposive sample of 200 outputs. Phase 3 involved semi-structured interviews with 60 university students who interacted with LLM-generated historical texts in think-aloud sessions.

The article makes three principal contributions. First, it provides the first integrated theoretical framework connecting Halbwachs's collective memory theory with the specific sociotechnical mechanisms of LLM text generation. Second, it presents the first systematic, cross-platform empirical analysis of LLM-generated historical narratives treated as memory texts. Third, it demonstrates through user reception analysis that LLMs introduce a novel mode of mnemonic socialization with significant implications for historical literacy and pluralistic memory cultures.

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the theoretical framework, connecting Halbwachs's collective memory with digital memory studies and critical algorithm studies to construct the Algorithmic Memory Framework. Section 3 details the mixed-methods research design. Section 4 presents the findings and analysis. Section 5 discusses implications and limitations. Section 6 concludes with reflections on the governance of algorithmic memory.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: From Social Frameworks of Memory to Algorithmic Frameworks

### 2.1 Halbwachs and the Social Construction of Memory

Maurice Halbwachs's work on collective memory, developed across his major writings from the 1920s through his posthumously published *La memoire collective* (1950), established the foundational insight that memory is irreducibly social. Against the psychological individualism of his contemporaries, Halbwachs argued that individuals never remember in isolation; rather, they remember within "social frameworks" (*cadres sociaux*) provided by the groups to which they belong—families, religious communities, social classes, and nations. These frameworks supply the categories, narratives, and evaluative schemas through which individual experiences become intelligible and communicable as memories. Memory, in Halbwachs's account, is not a private archive of personal experiences but a social practice continuously reconstructed in the present through interaction with others and with the cultural artifacts—texts, monuments, rituals, institutions—that embody shared understandings of the past.

Three dimensions of Halbwachs's theory are particularly relevant for understanding LLMs as cultural agents of memory. First, his concept of *mnemonic socialization*: the process through which individuals acquire, internalize, and reproduce the memory frameworks of their social groups. Halbwachs showed that this socialization occurs through everyday communicative practices—conversations, storytelling, shared reading, participation in commemorative rituals. The question this article poses is whether interaction with LLMs constitutes a new form of mnemonic socialization, and if so, what kind of memory frameworks it transmits.

Second, Halbwachs's insight that collective memory is *selective and reconstructive*: groups do not remember everything, but construct narratives of the past that serve present needs, values, and identities. This selectivity is not arbitrary but structured by relations of power—whose memories are preserved, whose are marginalized, and whose are erased reflects the social hierarchies of the remembering community. LLMs, as we will argue, introduce new modalities of selection and reconstruction that operate through computational rather than purely social processes.

Third, Halbwachs emphasized the *multiplicity* of collective memory: because individuals belong to multiple groups, they inhabit multiple, sometimes contradictory memory frameworks simultaneously. This multiplicity is what enables contestation, debate, and the pluralism of historical understanding that characterizes democratic societies. The question of whether LLMs tend to flatten this multiplicity—producing homogenized accounts that suppress historiographical plurality—is central to this article's inquiry.

### 2.2 Digital Memory Studies: From Archives to Platforms to AI

The digital turn has prompted extensive rethinking of Halbwachs's framework. Scholars of digital memory have traced how the affordances of digital technologies—their capacity for storage, reproduction, circulation, and algorithmic processing—transform the conditions under which collective memory is produced, mediated, and contested. This scholarship has evolved through several phases that are worth briefly reconstructing, as they provide the immediate intellectual context for the present study.

The first phase, emerging in the early 2000s, focused on digital archives and the unprecedented capacity of digital technologies to store and make accessible vast quantities of historical documentation. Scholars examined how digitization projects transformed access to the past, creating what some termed a "total archive" that promised to overcome the selectivity inherent in traditional archival practices. However, as critics noted, digitization introduced its own forms of selectivity: what was digitized, how it was organized, and who could access it remained shaped by institutional resources, linguistic hierarchies, and political priorities.

The second phase, gaining momentum from the mid-2010s, shifted attention to social media platforms as sites of collective remembering and forgetting. Adriaansen and Smit [2025] identify key developments in this literature, including concepts like "connective memory" and "memory of the multitude" that capture how platformization reshapes mnemonic practices through algorithmic curation. Makhortykh [2023] critically examines how commercial recommender systems influence remembrance through logics focused on maximizing engagement and profit, arguing that "contemporary memory infrastructures limit capabilities for forgetting on individual and collective levels." Keightley and Schlesinger [2024] describe a "colonisation of our personal and collective pasts for commercial benefit" through platformization. Hintz et al. [2025] emphasize that "forgetting demands renewed scholarly attention—whether through algorithmic omission, platform decay, or deliberate acts of erasure."

The third phase, now emerging, concerns AI systems—particularly generative AI and LLMs—as a qualitatively distinct development in the mediation of memory. Hoskins [2024] frames this as "a new battleground between humans and computers in the shaping of reality," arguing that AI platforms blur previously maintained distinctions between "implicit or explicit, unconscious or conscious, individual or collective" memory. Gensburger and Clavert [2024], in their special issue of *Memory Studies Review* asking whether AI is "the future of collective memory," observe that AI models "aggregate massive data about the past in a synthetic manner" and that their operation "enables information exploration more akin to human cognition than traditional archives." Hutson and Plate [2025] caution that "all AI systems sit within wider social, political, cultural and environmental assemblages" and that chatbots "work discriminatorily by silencing or misrepresenting minority groups."

What distinguishes LLMs from earlier digital memory technologies is their *generative* capacity. Digital archives preserve existing documents; social media platforms curate user-generated content; but LLMs produce novel texts that synthesize, interpolate, and recombine elements from their training corpora in response to user prompts. This generative capacity makes LLMs not merely *mediators* of pre-existing memory texts but *producers* of new ones—cultural agents that actively participate in the construction of historical narratives rather than simply facilitating access to them.

### 2.3 Critical Algorithm Studies and Discursive Power

A third theoretical strand informing this study draws on critical algorithm studies, particularly work examining how algorithmic processes function as mechanisms of discursive power. Mackay and Chia [2024] draw on Foucault's concept of "discursive formation" to analyze generative AI's influence, identifying a dual discourse: one emanating from the ideology of technological solutionism and another from the utterances generated by AI systems themselves, "shaped by various algorithmic, epistemic and linguistic influences." This Foucauldian lens illuminates how LLMs do not simply convey information but constitute a discursive regime—a system of rules, conventions, and power relations that determines what can be said, how it can be said, and with what authority.

The literature on LLM bias provides empirical substance to this theoretical claim. Tao et al. [2024], in their disaggregated evaluation of cultural bias across 107 countries, demonstrate that "LLMs trained on predominantly English text exhibit a latent bias favoring Western cultural values, especially when prompted in English." Tao et al. [2025] propose a framework for evaluating cultural bias and historical misconceptions in LLM outputs, finding that "training data disproportionately reflect Western-centric perspectives, resulting in generated content that amplifies dominant narratives while marginalizing underrepresented viewpoints." Navigli et al. [2023] provide a comprehensive inventory of social biases in LLM-generated text spanning gender, age, ethnicity, religion, and culture. De Vries and Sauer [2025] trace the "bias loop" in AI-driven cultural heritage, showing how "digital technologies both inherit and amplify existing societal and historical prejudices."

These findings establish that LLM-generated text is not a neutral representation of historical knowledge but a culturally positioned artifact that systematically privileges certain perspectives, narratives, and interpretive frameworks. However, what the bias literature lacks—and what this article aims to provide—is a theoretical framework connecting these empirical observations to the dynamics of collective memory.

### 2.4 The Algorithmic Memory Framework (AMF)

Synthesizing these three theoretical traditions, this article proposes the Algorithmic Memory Framework (AMF), which identifies four mechanisms through which LLMs reshape collective memory:

**Aggregative synthesis** describes the process by which LLMs compress vast textual corpora into probabilistic models that produce "averaged" accounts of the past. Because LLMs generate text based on statistical patterns across their training data, they tend to reproduce the most frequently occurring narratives, framings, and interpretations—effectively amplifying dominant perspectives while attenuating minority ones. This is not a matter of deliberate editorial choice but a structural property of statistical language modeling: the more frequently a narrative appears in the training data, the more likely it is to be reproduced.

**Presentist anchoring** captures how LLMs impose contemporary moral and epistemic frameworks on historical events. This mechanism reflects both the temporal composition of training data (which overrepresents recent texts) and the effects of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), through which models are aligned with contemporary values and sensibilities. The result is a systematic tendency to evaluate past events through present-day moral categories, obscuring the historical specificity of past worldviews, moral frameworks, and epistemic conditions.

**Epistemic flattening** describes how LLMs present historical narratives with uniform confidence, collapsing distinctions between well-established facts, contested interpretations, speculative claims, and active historiographical debates. Unlike academic historical writing, which carefully modulates epistemic claims through hedging, citation of sources, and acknowledgment of uncertainty, LLM-generated text typically presents all claims with the same authoritative register, making it difficult for users to distinguish between what is known, what is debated, and what is uncertain.

**Mnemonic hegemony** refers to the systematic reproduction of culturally dominant perspectives in LLM-generated historical narratives. Because training corpora overwhelmingly consist of English-language, Western, and Global North texts, LLMs encode and reproduce the cultural perspectives, narrative frameworks, and evaluative schemas dominant in these traditions. This creates what Yasseri [2025] calls a "standardization of collective memory"—a process by which diverse, locally rooted memory traditions are overwritten by globally circulating, algorithmically produced narratives that reflect the cultural perspectives of a narrow set of societies.

These four mechanisms are analytically distinct but empirically intertwined. Together, they constitute what we term *algorithmic frameworks of memory*—the sociotechnical equivalent of Halbwachs's social frameworks, structuring how users encounter, interpret, and internalize the past through their interactions with LLM systems.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Research Design

This study employs a sequential mixed-methods design combining computational text analysis with qualitative inquiry across three phases. The design reflects the study's dual aim of identifying systematic patterns in LLM-generated historical narratives (requiring computational analysis at scale) and understanding the meanings, discursive strategies, and reception dynamics of these texts (requiring interpretive, qualitative methods). The sequential structure allows findings from earlier phases to inform the design and sampling of later phases.

### 3.2 Phase 1: Systematic Prompt Experiment

Phase 1 involved a systematic prompt experiment designed to generate a comprehensive corpus of LLM-produced historical narratives for computational analysis.

**Event selection.** Six contested historical events were selected through purposive sampling designed to maximize geographical, temporal, and political diversity: (1) the Partition of India (1947), (2) the Transatlantic Slave Trade (16th-19th centuries), (3) the Nakba / establishment of Israel (1948), (4) the Rwandan Genocide (1994), (5) the atomic bombing of Hiroshima (1945), and (6) European colonization of the Americas (15th-19th centuries). These events were selected because each involves multiple legitimate perspectives, active historiographical debate, and ongoing political contestation—making them ideal for examining how LLMs handle the multiplicity and contestation that characterize collective memory.

**Platform selection.** Three major LLM platforms were selected: OpenAI's GPT-4 (accessed via API), Anthropic's Claude 3 (accessed via API), and Google's Gemini Pro (accessed via API). These were chosen as the three most widely used commercial LLM platforms, each developed by different organizations with different training data, alignment procedures, and corporate cultures, enabling cross-platform comparison.

**Prompt design.** A standardized battery of 30 prompts per event (180 total) was developed, organized into five categories: (a) factual recall prompts ("What happened during X?"), (b) causal explanation prompts ("What caused X?"), (c) perspective-taking prompts ("How do different groups understand X?"), (d) moral evaluation prompts ("Was X justified?"), and (e) legacy prompts ("What is the lasting significance of X?"). Each prompt was issued in four languages—English, Spanish, Mandarin Chinese, and Arabic—generating a total corpus of 2,160 text outputs (30 prompts x 6 events x 3 platforms x 4 languages, with some attrition due to content filtering refusals).

**Computational analysis.** The corpus was analyzed using a suite of computational text analysis methods: (a) topic modeling (LDA) to identify thematic patterns and cross-platform convergences; (b) sentiment analysis to examine evaluative framing; (c) named entity recognition to track which actors, groups, and places are foregrounded or marginalized; (d) custom-developed measures of *narrative diversity* (the range of perspectives represented within a single response) and *epistemic hedging* (the frequency and type of uncertainty markers, qualifications, and acknowledgments of debate); and (e) cross-linguistic comparison of outputs for the same prompts across languages.

### 3.3 Phase 2: Qualitative Discourse Analysis

Phase 2 involved critical discourse analysis (CDA) of a purposive sample of 200 LLM-generated texts selected from the Phase 1 corpus for maximum variation across platforms, events, prompt types, and languages. Following Fairclough's three-dimensional model, analysis examined: (a) *textual features*—lexical choices, syntactic structures, narrative sequencing, voice and agency attribution; (b) *discursive practices*—how texts construct epistemic authority, manage competing perspectives, and position the reader; and (c) *social practices*—how textual and discursive features connect to broader power relations, cultural hegemonies, and memory politics. Two researchers independently coded the sample, achieving inter-rater reliability of Cohen's kappa = 0.81, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

### 3.4 Phase 3: User Reception Study

Phase 3 involved semi-structured interviews with 60 university students (20 per country) recruited from universities in the United Kingdom, Mexico, and Egypt. Countries were selected to represent different positions in the global knowledge hierarchy: a major English-speaking country whose perspectives are overrepresented in LLM training data, a major Spanish-speaking country in the Global South, and a major Arabic-speaking country with distinct historiographical traditions. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling to ensure diversity of academic discipline (humanities, social sciences, natural sciences), gender, and prior experience with LLM tools.

Interviews followed a semi-structured protocol incorporating think-aloud procedures: participants were presented with LLM-generated texts about two contested historical events (one with direct relevance to their national context, one without) and asked to read, evaluate, and discuss them while articulating their thinking process. Follow-up questions probed participants' assessment of the texts' accuracy, completeness, fairness, and authority; their awareness of potential biases; their comparison with other sources of historical knowledge; and their reflections on how LLM use might shape their understanding of history.

Interviews were conducted in participants' preferred language (English, Spanish, or Arabic), transcribed, and translated into English for analysis. Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke's reflexive approach, generating themes through iterative coding by two researchers. Ethical approval was obtained from the lead institution's research ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent.

### 3.5 Limitations

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, the prompt experiment captures LLM outputs at a specific moment in time; given that models are updated regularly, outputs may differ at different time points. Second, the user reception study involves university students, who represent a particular demographic with relatively high digital and historical literacy; findings may not generalize to other populations. Third, the four languages studied, while representing significant global diversity, do not capture the full range of linguistic and cultural contexts in which LLMs are used. Fourth, the study focuses on factual and interpretive prompts about well-known historical events; LLMs' treatment of obscure, local, or indigenous histories may exhibit different patterns.

## 4. Analysis and Discussion

### 4.1 Aggregative Synthesis and the Flattening of Historical Plurality

The computational analysis reveals a striking pattern of narrative convergence across all three LLM platforms. When queried about the same historical events, GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini produce texts that share remarkably similar thematic structures, causal frameworks, and evaluative orientations—despite differences in their training data, alignment procedures, and corporate contexts. Topic modeling identifies a core set of themes for each event that appears consistently across platforms: for the Partition of India, for instance, all three platforms foreground British colonial administration, Hindu-Muslim communal tensions, and mass displacement, while consistently backgrounding or omitting subaltern perspectives, the role of princely states, and the experiences of women and lower-caste communities.

This convergence supports the AMF's concept of aggregative synthesis: because all three models are trained on overlapping corpora dominated by English-language academic and journalistic texts, they converge on the narrative frameworks most prevalent in those corpora. The result is a form of narrative homogenization that operates not through deliberate editorial choices but through the statistical logic of language modeling itself. Narrative diversity scores—measuring the range of perspectives, interpretations, and voices represented within individual responses—are significantly lower for LLM-generated texts than for comparable human-authored texts in academic history journals (mean LLM narrative diversity score = 2.3/10; mean academic text score = 6.8/10, p < 0.001).

The qualitative discourse analysis deepens this finding by revealing *how* homogenization operates at the textual level. LLM-generated texts characteristically employ what we term a "balanced overview" register—a discursive strategy that presents multiple perspectives not as genuinely competing interpretations demanding engagement, but as items in a catalogue to be acknowledged and then subsumed into a synthesized, supposedly neutral account. For example, when asked about the Nakba, all three models produce texts that acknowledge both Palestinian and Israeli perspectives but frame them within a "balanced" narrative structure that implicitly positions the model as an authoritative arbiter above the fray—a position that no human historian could credibly claim. This "view from nowhere" [Nagel 1986] is precisely the kind of epistemic positioning that Halbwachs's framework helps us recognize as itself socially constructed: the appearance of neutrality masks a particular cultural stance—one rooted in Western liberal norms of objectivity, balance, and both-sides-ism.

### 4.2 Presentist Anchoring and the Imposition of Contemporary Frameworks

Analysis reveals a systematic tendency across all platforms to evaluate historical events through contemporary moral categories. When asked about European colonization of the Americas, all three models consistently frame their responses through present-day concepts such as "indigenous rights," "cultural genocide," "systemic racism," and "environmental destruction"—categories that, while analytically powerful, are anachronistic when applied without qualification to 16th- and 17th-century contexts. The models rarely attempt to reconstruct the moral universes within which historical actors operated, instead imposing a retroactive moral clarity that flattens the genuine moral complexity and strangeness of the past.

This presentist anchoring is particularly evident in the models' treatment of moral evaluation prompts. When asked whether the atomic bombing of Hiroshima was justified, all three models produce responses structured around a contemporary humanitarian framework that emphasizes civilian casualties and international law, while struggling to reconstruct the strategic, political, and moral considerations that shaped decision-making in 1945. The historical specificity of the past—the particular configurations of knowledge, belief, power, and material constraint within which historical actors operated—is consistently subordinated to present-day evaluative frameworks.

The mechanism underlying presentist anchoring appears to be twofold. First, the temporal composition of LLM training data skews heavily toward recent texts, which naturally embed contemporary perspectives. Second, RLHF alignment processes—through which models are trained to produce responses that human evaluators rate as helpful, harmless, and honest—systematically privilege contemporary moral sensibilities, since evaluators themselves are embedded in present-day moral frameworks. The result is what Jan Assmann might call a failure of *cultural memory*: the capacity to imaginatively inhabit past worlds, to understand the past on its own terms, is systematically suppressed in favor of what we might call *moral presentism*.

### 4.3 Epistemic Flattening and the Suppression of Uncertainty

Perhaps the most consequential finding concerns what the AMF terms epistemic flattening: the suppression of uncertainty, debate, and historiographical plurality in LLM-generated historical texts. Computational analysis of epistemic hedging reveals that LLM-generated texts employ uncertainty markers (hedges such as "perhaps," "arguably," "some historians contend," "this remains debated") at rates approximately one-third those found in comparable academic historical texts (mean hedging frequency per 1,000 words: LLM outputs = 3.2; academic texts = 9.7, p < 0.001).

More importantly, the qualitative analysis reveals that even when LLM-generated texts do acknowledge historiographical debate, they characteristically do so in a formulaic, performative manner that contains and neutralizes disagreement rather than genuinely engaging with it. A typical pattern involves acknowledging that "historians debate" a particular question, briefly listing two or three positions, and then either implicitly siding with one (usually the majority Western academic position) or retreating to a vague synthesis that dissolves the substantive content of the disagreement. This discursive strategy creates an appearance of pluralism while substantively foreclosing the openness and contestation that characterize genuine historiographical debate.

The implications for collective memory are significant. As Halbwachs emphasized, the multiplicity of memory frameworks—the coexistence of competing accounts of the past within a society—is what enables democratic contestation over the meaning of history. When LLMs flatten this multiplicity into apparently authoritative, singular accounts, they potentially undermine the conditions for pluralistic memory cultures. This is not a matter of LLMs getting history "wrong" in the sense of stating factual inaccuracies (though this also occurs); it is a matter of LLMs getting the *epistemic status* of historical knowledge wrong—presenting as settled what is genuinely debated, and as neutral what is culturally positioned.

### 4.4 Mnemonic Hegemony and Cross-Linguistic Asymmetries

Cross-linguistic analysis reveals significant asymmetries that support the AMF's concept of mnemonic hegemony. When the same prompts are issued in different languages, the resulting texts exhibit systematically different perspectives—but not in ways that reflect the historiographical traditions of the language communities. Rather, English-language outputs set the baseline, and outputs in other languages tend to converge toward this baseline while exhibiting occasional, inconsistent deviations.

For example, when asked about the Transatlantic Slave Trade in English, all three models produce narratives centered on the African-American experience, foregrounding the Middle Passage, plantation slavery in the American South, and the abolitionist movement. When the same prompts are issued in Spanish, outputs partially shift toward including Latin American slavery but retain the dominant framework established in English. When issued in Arabic, outputs add brief mentions of the Arab slave trade but frame it predominantly through the Euro-American narrative structure. The overall pattern suggests that English-language historiographical frameworks function as a default or "anchor" that shapes outputs in other languages—a form of cultural hegemony operating through the statistical structure of multilingual training data.

This finding aligns with Tao et al.'s [2024] observation that LLMs "exhibit a latent bias favoring Western cultural values, especially when prompted in English," but extends it by demonstrating that this bias operates not just at the level of cultural values but at the level of historical narrative structure—determining which events are foregrounded, which actors are given agency, which causal frameworks are deployed, and which moral evaluations are applied.

### 4.5 User Reception: Algorithmic Mnemonic Socialization

The user reception study reveals concerning patterns in how participants engage with LLM-generated historical texts. Across all three national contexts, participants demonstrate a strong tendency to treat LLM outputs as authoritative accounts of historical events. When asked to evaluate the accuracy and reliability of LLM-generated texts, 73% of participants (44/60) rated them as "accurate" or "mostly accurate" without prompting, and only 18% (11/60) spontaneously questioned the epistemic status or potential biases of the texts.

Particularly striking is the finding that participants who did identify limitations in LLM-generated texts tended to focus on factual omissions ("it didn't mention X") rather than structural or perspectival biases ("it presents this from a Western perspective" or "it treats this as settled when it's actually debated"). This suggests that users engage with LLM-generated historical texts primarily in an informational mode—evaluating them for factual completeness—rather than a critical-interpretive mode that would attend to framing, perspective, and epistemic positioning.

However, significant cross-national differences emerged. Egyptian participants were substantially more likely than British or Mexican participants to identify Western-centric framing in texts about the Nakba and European colonialism (55% vs. 20% and 25%, respectively). This suggests that users whose own historical experiences are most visibly marginalized in LLM-generated accounts are also most attuned to the biases these accounts encode—a finding consistent with standpoint epistemology and with research on marginalized communities' critical media literacy.

The think-aloud protocols reveal a further dynamic: when participants interacted with LLMs through follow-up questions and prompts, they frequently found that the models adjusted their responses to accommodate the user's framing—a pattern we term *responsive alignment*. While this might appear to support pluralism, the qualitative analysis suggests it functions more as an echo-chamber effect: the model tells users what they seem to want to hear, reinforcing rather than challenging their existing frameworks. Several participants noted, with varying degrees of awareness, that "it just agrees with whatever angle I push it toward" (Participant UK-14). This responsive alignment complicates the picture of LLMs as imposing a single hegemonic narrative; rather, their influence may operate through a more subtle dynamic in which initial outputs establish a framework that users then modify through interaction, but within parameters set by the model's aggregative synthesis.

### 4.6 Theoretical Implications: Algorithmic Frameworks of Memory

Taken together, these findings support the article's central argument: LLMs function as cultural agents of memory that introduce a new mode of mnemonic socialization operating through algorithmic frameworks. These algorithmic frameworks differ from Halbwachs's social frameworks in several important respects.

First, they operate at unprecedented scale: a single model can serve hundreds of millions of users simultaneously, potentially producing a degree of narrative convergence that no previous memory institution could achieve. Yasseri [2025] rightly identifies this as a risk of "standardization of collective memory"—but our findings suggest the process is more complex than simple standardization, involving dynamic interactions between model outputs, user prompts, and responsive alignment.

Second, algorithmic frameworks are *opaque* in ways that social frameworks are not. When an individual absorbs the memory frameworks of their family, school, or national culture, the social sources of these frameworks are at least potentially identifiable. But when an individual absorbs historical narratives from an LLM, the sources—billions of documents compressed into statistical patterns—are fundamentally unrecoverable. This opacity undermines the possibility of critical engagement with the frameworks themselves.

Third, algorithmic frameworks combine *authority* with *responsiveness* in a novel way. LLMs present their outputs with the authoritative register of an encyclopedia while simultaneously adjusting their claims in response to user pushback—a combination that, as our user reception analysis suggests, is particularly effective at naturalizing the frameworks they transmit.

These properties make LLMs what Smit [2024] might recognize as a new materialization of collective memory's theoretical properties—but one with significantly amplified consequences for the distribution of mnemonic power in global information ecosystems.

## 5. Conclusion

This article has argued that large language models function as cultural agents of memory that actively reshape collective memory and historical narratives in the digital public sphere. Through the development of the Algorithmic Memory Framework and mixed-methods empirical analysis, we have demonstrated that LLM-generated historical texts exhibit systematic tendencies toward aggregative synthesis, presentist anchoring, epistemic flattening, and mnemonic hegemony—tendencies that, taken together, risk undermining the pluralistic memory cultures that democratic societies depend upon.

These findings carry urgent implications for multiple stakeholders. For LLM developers, they underscore the need to treat historical narrative generation not as a generic text production task but as a culturally consequential practice requiring careful attention to perspectival diversity, epistemic humility, and the representation of historiographical debate. For educators, they highlight the importance of developing pedagogies that equip students to engage critically with AI-generated historical accounts—attending not just to factual accuracy but to framing, perspective, and epistemic status. For policymakers, they suggest that the governance of AI systems must attend to their role as memory technologies—not merely as information systems—and consider the implications for cultural diversity, historical literacy, and democratic deliberation.

As Gensburger and Clavert [2024] ask: is artificial intelligence the future of collective memory? Our analysis suggests that this question is not merely speculative but already being answered in practice, every time a user turns to ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini to learn about the past. The challenge for scholars, developers, and citizens alike is to ensure that this answer preserves the multiplicity, contestation, and cultural diversity that make collective memory a living, democratic practice rather than a standardized, algorithmically produced commodity.

The Halbwachsian insight with which we began—that memory is social, selective, and multiple—remains as relevant as ever, but must now be extended to encompass the algorithmic agencies that increasingly mediate our relationship to the past. Understanding LLMs as cultural agents of memory is a first step toward ensuring that these powerful technologies serve the purposes of democratic remembrance rather than its homogenization.
